### A Hypothesis of Music-Based Brain–Body Regulation-An Integrated Model of Acoustic Structure, Prediction, Bodily Synchronization, Spatial Perception, and Immersion

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*FnW4hzpSuD1PZJqrj1IXsA.png)

### 0. Status and Purpose of This Document

This document is a working hypothesis about how specific musical structures may influence cognition, bodily states, emotion, attention, arousal, and immersive experience.

Some components are compatible with established findings in music cognition, auditory perception, neuroscience, and motor synchronization. Other components are hypotheses derived primarily from personal phenomenological observations.

These should not be treated as equivalent.

The purpose of this document is not to establish a finished theory, but to decompose the idea of “music regulating the brain and body” into potentially testable musical parameters.

### 1. The Initial Observation

When humans listen to music, they do not merely understand the meaning of the song.

Musical structures themselves can produce experiences such as:

* bodily movement or an urge to move
* changes in breathing or bodily rhythm
* tension and relaxation
* a sense of spatial expansion
* immersion
* increased mental clarity
* emotional activation
* anticipation
* a feeling that the body is “coming online”

These responses do not necessarily require lyrical or narrative meaning.

This suggests a possible pathway:

**Acoustic structure → nervous system / body → subjective experience**

Music may therefore function not only as a medium for communicating meaning, but also as a structured stimulus capable of directly modulating perceptual, motor, emotional, and bodily processes.

### 2. Music Should Not Be Treated as a Single “Emotion” Variable

It is common to describe music using global labels such as:

* sad
* happy
* exciting
* emotional
* relaxing

However, this model treats musical response as a collection of partially distinct pathways.

Possible pathways include:

1. Harmonic and predictive processing
2. Rhythmic and bodily synchronization
3. Timbre and auditory-sensory processing
4. Spatial and bodily-boundary perception
5. Memory and semantic association
6. Emotional evaluation
7. Motor activation and arousal

These pathways are not independent. They interact continuously.

Therefore, two pieces that produce a similar subjective emotion may do so through substantially different underlying mechanisms.

### 3. Central Hypothesis: Music Can Selectively Drive Different Pathways

Music can be understood as:

**a stimulus that temporally organizes multiple neural and bodily pathways.**

For example:

**Sound → prediction → maintained expectation → subtle violation → tension → resolution**

may preferentially engage cognitive and emotional processing.

By contrast:

**Sound → periodicity → temporal prediction → motor synchronization → bodily activation**

may preferentially engage bodily and motor processes.

Another pathway might be:

**Sound → spatial information → auditory expansion → bodily boundary perception → immersion**

Thus, instead of simply asking:

> *“How strongly does this music affect me?”*

we can ask:

> *“Which pathway is being affected, and how?”*

### 4. Harmony and Melody: Effects on Cognitive Processing

In preliminary generative-music testing, the harmonic version produced the strongest subjective impression of “affecting the brain.”

One possible explanation is the predictive structure created by harmony.

When listening to music, the listener may implicitly predict what sound or chord will occur next.

A musical structure can therefore create:

**prediction → slight deviation → tension → continued expectation → resolution**

Potentially effective musical elements include:

* semitone or whole-tone movement
* narrow melodic intervals
* minor-second tension
* suspended notes
* incomplete cadences
* delayed resolution
* rising melodic phrases
* descending melodic phrases
* recurring motifs with small variations
* chromatic or stepwise movement in inner voices

The important factor may not simply be “complex harmony,” but the controlled manipulation of expectation.

### 5. Voice Leading May Matter More Than Chord Labels

A chord progression can be described using chord names such as:

C → Am → F → G

However, subjective experience may depend strongly on how individual voices move between those chords.

For example, if one voice remains almost stationary while another moves by a semitone, the listener may experience a subtle sense of motion that cannot be fully described by the chord labels themselves.

This suggests a distinction between:

**Chord progression**

and

**the temporal movement of individual pitches.**

The latter may contribute to an auditory analogue of movement or bodily sensation.

### 6. Tension and Release

One fundamental structure is:

**tension → suspension → release**

The important factor may be the change between states rather than the absolute amount of tension.

A continuously dissonant piece may eventually become perceptually normalized.

By contrast:

**stability → instability → expectation → resolution**

creates a dynamic contrast.

Therefore, musical stimulation may be better understood as the design of **state transitions**, rather than the maximization of stimulation.

### 7. Rhythm: The Bodily Synchronization Pathway

Rhythm may engage a different pathway from harmony.

When a listener hears a periodic pattern, the motor and perceptual systems can predict the timing of future events.

A possible sequence is:

**periodicity → temporal prediction → motor prediction → bodily synchronization**

This may explain why rhythmic music can produce an experience of the body “coming online.”

In the preliminary experiment, a rhythm-focused version produced a noticeable feeling that the body was becoming activated.

Interestingly, this was accompanied by an impression of increased mental clarity rather than increased cognitive clutter.

### 8. Bodily Activation and Cognitive Stimulation May Be Separate Axes

A common assumption is:

**stronger stimulation = stronger cognitive activation**

But the preliminary observation suggests that these variables may be partially separable.

It may be possible to have:

**bodily activation ↑**

while simultaneously having:

**cognitive clutter ↓**

Possible explanations include:

* stabilization of motor timing
* reduction of unnecessary motor preparation
* more stable attentional anchoring
* improved coupling between perception and movement
* reduced internal fluctuation

Thus, music might sometimes increase bodily activation without increasing subjective cognitive load.

This is an important hypothesis for future testing.

### 9. Timbre and Spatial Processing: The Immersion Pathway

Spatial audio processing may produce effects distinct from those produced by harmony or rhythm.

Relevant parameters include:

* reverberation
* stereo width
* perceived distance
* depth
* lateral movement
* diffusion
* frequency-dependent spatial distribution

Dry sounds tend to feel localized:

> *“The sound is there.”*

Long reverberation and spatial diffusion can instead create:

> *“The sound exists around me.”*

This may contribute to:

**acoustic space → spatial perception → bodily boundary perception → immersion**

The relationship between acoustic space and bodily self-perception remains a hypothesis.

### 10. Spatial Processing and Bodily Synchronization

The preliminary observations suggested different subjective effects:

**Spatial version:**
greater spatial expansion and immersive qualities.

**Rhythmic synchronization version:**
stronger bodily activation and a clearer mental state.

A tentative distinction is:

**Spatial processing → external space / bodily boundary**

**Rhythmic synchronization → internal movement / arousal**

These should not be treated as established neurological divisions. They are useful working categories for experimentation.

### 11. Timbre

Timbre should not be treated simply as “which instrument is playing.”

Relevant acoustic properties include:

* attack
* decay
* sustain
* release
* harmonic spectrum
* noise components
* frequency distribution
* temporal density

Soft pads and strings can blur the boundaries of individual sounds and create continuous, spatial experiences.

Piano and percussion have clearer attacks and therefore stronger temporal definition.

A combination of:

**soft background textures + clear transient events**

may provide both:

* spatial continuity
* bodily / temporal clarity

### 12. Bass

Low-frequency content may play an important role in bodily experience.

Potentially relevant features include:

* deep bass
* smooth bass lines
* descending bass movement
* ascending bass movement
* pedal tones

Descending bass lines may subjectively suggest:

* grounding
* sinking
* settling

Ascending bass lines may suggest:

* rising
* lifting
* activation

A pedal tone creates another interesting structure:

**stable low-frequency foundation + changing upper harmony**

This may allow the listener to experience a stable bodily base while cognitive or harmonic space changes above it.

### 13. Silence and Absence of Sound

Stimulation does not require continuous sound.

Silence can create:

**expectation → anticipation → absence → renewed input**

A sudden reduction in sound after a dense passage can make the silence itself perceptually salient.

For example:

**dense harmony + high energy → sudden silence → minimal sound**

can create a strong state transition.

Thus, musical design should include both the presence and absence of sound.

### 14. Musical Development as State Transition

Musical development should not be understood simply as:

**quiet → loud → climax**

Instead, it can be modeled as:

**state A → expectation → state B → tension → state C → integration → release**

A potentially useful structure is:

1. Quiet beginning
2. Small recurring motif
3. Subtle variation
4. Accumulation of expectation
5. Introduction of bodily rhythm
6. Harmonic expansion
7. Expansion of spatial field
8. Controlled climax
9. Sudden reduction
10. Spacious afterglow

### 15. The Goal Is Integration Rather Than Maximum Stimulation

A central refinement of the model is that the objective should not be:

> *“Stimulate the brain and body as strongly as possible.”*

Instead, the goal may be:

* moderate cognitive engagement
* appropriate bodily activation
* coherent spatial perception
* reduced interference between pathways

This produces a hypothetical sequence:

**stimulation → synchronization → reduced friction → clarity**

The optimal musical state may therefore not correspond to maximum intensity.

### 16. A Preliminary Brain–Body Regulation Model

A simplified model is:

### Harmony

Prediction → tension → resolution
↓
Cognitive and emotional activation

### Rhythm

Periodicity → temporal prediction → synchronization
↓
Bodily activation and arousal

### Bass

Low-frequency foundation and movement
↓
Bodily grounding

### Timbre

Harmonic spectrum and temporal texture
↓
Sensory quality

### Spatial processing

Distance, width, reverberation
↓
Immersion and bodily-boundary perception

### Musical development

State transitions
↓
Integration and release

These processes interact rather than operate independently.

### 17. The Concept of “Pathways”

A particularly important personal observation is the distinction between:

**a response disappearing**

and

**a pathway becoming less frequently activated.**

A person may experience a substantial reduction in the spontaneous bodily or emotional response normally associated with music, while the underlying pathway remains available.

A sufficiently specific stimulus may then reactivate it.

This suggests a model in which different pathways have different activation thresholds:

* Pathway A: easily activated
* Pathway B: weakly activated
* Pathway C: rarely reaches conscious awareness
* Pathway D: strongly activated only by specific stimuli

Music may therefore function as a tool for selectively probing these pathways.

### 18. Why AI-Generated Music Is Interesting

AI-generated music provides an interesting experimental environment.

Human composers often create music through personal intention, expression, memory, and subjective experience.

Generative models can instead reproduce statistical regularities learned from large amounts of music.

These regularities include:

* harmonic progressions
* melodic patterns
* rhythmic structures
* timbral combinations
* transitions
* climaxes
* resolutions

Therefore, AI-generated music may sometimes produce highly concentrated combinations of musical structures that humans tend to respond to.

This does not mean that an AI “understands human emotion.”

A more conservative interpretation is:

> *AI systems can statistically reconstruct musical structures that frequently occur in music that humans find perceptually and emotionally compelling.*

### 19. AI Music as a Pathway-Exploration Tool

Generative music can therefore be used as an exploratory tool.

The question becomes:

> *“Which musical parameters activate which subjective pathway?”*

For example:

### Condition A

Change harmony while keeping rhythm, lyrics, tempo, and arrangement similar.

### Condition B

Change rhythm while keeping harmony similar.

### Condition C

Change timbre.

### Condition D

Change spatial processing.

This allows the listener to compare subjective responses while controlling some variables.

Possible measurements include:

* bodily activation
* mental clarity
* emotional intensity
* immersion
* spatial expansion
* urge to move
* tension
* relaxation
* attentional stability
* subjective cognitive load

Possible contributors:

Possible contributors:

### 21. Genre as an Outer Layer

Musical genre can be treated as an “outer layer” rather than as the fundamental mechanism.

The same underlying regulation principles can potentially be implemented in different genres.

Examples:

**Ambient**

* spatial immersion
* clarity
* gradual expansion

**Jazz**

* harmonic prediction
* subtle variation
* complex voice leading

**House**

* bodily synchronization
* repetition
* pulse

**Post-rock**

* long-term accumulation
* expansion
* release

**Shoegaze**

* spatial immersion
* dense timbral fields

**Speedcore**

* extremely fast bodily synchronization
* rapid harmonic tension and release
* high-energy spatial openness

The genre changes the surface expression while the underlying mechanisms may remain similar.

### 22. Speedcore as an Extreme Test Case

Simply increasing BPM may produce:

**stimulation → fatigue / pressure**

rather than clarity.

A more interesting configuration is:

**very fast rhythm**
+
**bright harmony**
+
**clear high-frequency transients**
+
**deep but controlled bass**
+
**rapid tension and resolution**
+
**wide spatial field**

The goal is not aggressive intensity, but:

> *high-speed movement that feels light, exhilarating, lucid, and physically energizing.*

This can be conceptualized as:

**high-speed bodily synchronization + harmonic release + spatial openness**

### 23. Why Minimize Lyrical Meaning?

Lyrics introduce strong semantic, autobiographical, and emotional associations.

If the goal is to study the effects of acoustic structure itself, semantic content should be minimized.

Possible strategies include:

* meaningless syllables
* invented words
* vowel-rich vocalization
* short repeated phrases
* vocals treated as an instrument

Using Latin, Sanskrit, or other real languages introduces additional semantic and cultural associations.

For controlled experimentation, invented phonetic material is therefore preferable.

### 24. Connection to Cognitive Load and Secondary Reaction

This model can be connected to a broader distinction between:

**primary processing**

and

**secondary reaction to that processing.**

In music:

**sound → perception**

may be followed by:

**perception → evaluation → self-reference → additional cognitive activity**

The proposed ideal is not necessarily to suppress perception.

Instead, it may be possible to create music that:

* establishes useful perceptual and bodily activity
* supports synchronization
* maintains attention
* minimizes unnecessary secondary cognitive reaction

This would correspond to:

**active perception without excessive secondary processing.**

### 25. Connection to Flow

The bodily synchronization observations may have some conceptual overlap with flow.

Flow is often associated with:

* focused attention
* coupling between action and perception
* altered time perception
* reduced self-evaluation
* highly efficient action

In music:

**rhythm → prediction → synchronization → preparation for the next event → continuous perception/action coupling**

could potentially create a related low-friction processing state.

However, rhythmic synchronization and flow should not be treated as identical concepts.

### 26. Overall Integrated Hypothesis

The most comprehensive current hypothesis is:

> *Music is not merely a carrier of meaning. It is a temporally organized stimulus capable of selectively engaging predictive processing, emotion, bodily synchronization, spatial perception, and arousal.*

A further hypothesis is:

> *An optimal musical experience may not be one in which stimulation is maximized, but one in which multiple pathways are activated with appropriate intensity and timing while minimizing interference between them.*

Under this model:

**Harmony**
→ cognitive prediction and emotional processing

**Rhythm**
→ bodily synchronization and arousal

**Bass**
→ bodily grounding

**Timbre**
→ sensory texture

**Spatial processing**
→ immersion and bodily-boundary perception

**Musical development**
→ state transitions

The ultimate target is not maximum stimulation, but a possible state characterized by:

**Mind**

* lucid
* engaged
* spacious

**Body**

* awake
* grounded
* coordinated

**Attention**

* naturally stable

**Spatial experience**

* open

**Emotion**

* present without becoming excessively fixed

### 27. Future Experimental Questions

1. How does changing only harmony affect subjective experience?
2. How does changing only rhythm affect subjective experience?
3. How does timbre alter bodily versus cognitive effects?
4. How does spatial processing alter immersion?
5. Can the same underlying structure produce different effects in different genres?
6. How does BPM affect bodily activation and cognitive clarity?
7. How does harmonic complexity relate to the subjective feeling of “brain stimulation”?
8. Is stronger bodily synchronization associated with greater subjective mental clarity?
9. What happens when semantic meaning is completely removed from lyrics?
10. Does the effect diminish with prolonged listening?
11. Does repetition increase prediction and therefore change the subjective experience over time?
12. Do AI-generated songs and human-composed songs produce different effects when their musical structures are matched?
13. Can individual “pathways” be identified through repeated controlled listening experiments?
14. Can musical parameters be systematically optimized for a desired brain–body state?

### 28. Important Limitations

This is currently a hypothesis model grounded partly in personal phenomenological observation.

It should not be interpreted as establishing simple mappings such as:

> *“This chord activates this brain region.”*

Nor does it establish that music can reliably “optimize the brain and body.”

A more appropriate scientific question is:

> *How do changes in specific musical parameters alter subjective cognitive, bodily, emotional, and attentional states?*

The distinction between phenomenological observation and established neurophysiological mechanism should be maintained throughout future investigation.

### 29. One-Sentence Summary

Music can be understood not only as a carrier of meaning, but as a structured temporal stimulus that coordinates prediction, tension, release, bodily synchronization, spatial perception, and arousal across multiple cognitive–bodily pathways.

The most interesting possibility is that music may be used not simply to increase stimulation, but to activate the brain and body in complementary ways while reducing friction between them, potentially producing a state that is simultaneously **clear, awake, grounded, spacious, and integrated**.
