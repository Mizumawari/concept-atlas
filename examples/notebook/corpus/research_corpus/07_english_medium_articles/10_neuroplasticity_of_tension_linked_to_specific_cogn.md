### Neuroplasticity of Tension Linked to Specific Cognitions, Focal Dystonia, Yips, Performance Tension, and Everyday Conditioned Responses

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*To2y20B2sNA7l5s4cKCcMQ.png)

### 1. The Basic Question

Focal dystonia, yips, excessive tension during sports or musical performance, and everyday experiences such as suddenly becoming physically tense in specific situations are not necessarily the same phenomenon.

They can involve different neurological and psychological mechanisms.

However, they may share a broader structure:

**A specific cognition, situation, or prediction can become associated with a specific bodily response through long-term learning.**

From this perspective, excessive tension is not necessarily just an unwanted physical reaction. It can potentially become part of an automated learned pattern.
[https://medium.com/media/0abc087f2e8e5f95914eb282794450ba/href](https://medium.com/media/0abc087f2e8e5f95914eb282794450ba/href)

### 2. The Basic Hypothesis: Tension Itself Can Be Learned

Normally, a movement might follow a relatively simple sequence:

**Recognize the movement → activate the motor program → move**

But if excessive force, muscular contraction, attentional monitoring, pressure, or physiological arousal repeatedly accompany that movement, another pattern may gradually develop:

**Recognize the movement → become tense → perform the movement**

The important point is that tension can potentially become **attached to the movement itself**.

For example, in piano playing:

> *“Move this finger”
↓
Excessive tension in the finger or hand
↓
Play the note*

If this combination is repeated over a long period, the relationship between the movement representation and the tension response may become increasingly strong.

The same principle could potentially appear in sports:

> *“Throw the ball in this situation”
↓
Excessive contraction of the arm or shoulder
↓
Throw*

Or in everyday life:

> *“Speak in front of people”
↓
Throat and body become tense
↓
Speak*

The situation itself can eventually become a trigger for the bodily response.

### 3. The Reaction Can Become Automatic

As a response becomes sufficiently learned, the person does not necessarily consciously decide to become tense before the body reacts.

Motor preparation begins before conscious awareness of the action is fully formed.

Therefore, subjectively, someone may experience:

> *“As soon as I tried to do it, my body locked up.”*

But the nervous system may already have begun preparing the body before the person consciously experiences the intention to act.

This creates an important distinction.

The problem may not simply be:

**“I consciously became tense.”**

It may instead be:

**“The recognition or prediction of the situation automatically triggered the tension response.”**

This also explains why simply telling someone:

> *“Don’t tense up.”*

may not be sufficient.

### 4. The Critical Point Is the Beginning of the Prediction

Rather than looking only at the muscles, it is useful to ask:

**What causes the tension response to begin?**

For example:

* playing the piano
* throwing a ball
* speaking in public
* talking to a particular person
* performing an important task
* entering a high-pressure situation

The trigger may be the recognition or prediction of the situation itself.

The structure becomes:

**Cognition → bodily response**

Once sufficiently automated, the bodily response can appear before the actual movement begins.

Therefore, the problem is not necessarily just muscular tension.

It can involve the interaction between:

* cognition
* prediction
* motor preparation
* sensory processing
* autonomic arousal
* muscular activity
* learned motor patterns

### 5. Neuroplasticity and Thresholds

Neuroplastic changes do not necessarily become consciously noticeable little by little.

A person may spend years:

* repeating the same movement
* using the body in the same way
* repeatedly applying excessive force
* monitoring the movement intensely
* performing under pressure
* practicing while fatigued

Eventually, a threshold may be crossed.

From the person’s perspective, this can feel like:

> *“I was completely fine yesterday, and suddenly I couldn’t do it today.”*

But the underlying changes may have accumulated gradually, with the visible symptom appearing only after a certain threshold was reached.

Thus, the apparent sudden onset does not necessarily mean that the underlying learning occurred suddenly.

### 6. The Problem of Repeating the Abnormal Pattern

Once an abnormal tension pattern has developed, continuing to perform the same movement while that pattern is active may provide additional opportunities to reinforce it.

This creates a potential problem:

> *“I can’t do it properly, so I need to practice even more.”*

More practice is not automatically better if the practice repeatedly reinforces the same dysfunctional pattern.

The important question becomes:

**What state is being practiced?**

Not merely:

**How many repetitions are being performed?**

This distinction is particularly important when thinking about focal dystonia and other movement disorders.

### 7. Focal Dystonia

Focal dystonia is a neurological movement disorder in which involuntary abnormal postures or movements occur, often in specific contexts or during specific actions.

In musicians, for example, symptoms may include finger curling, unwanted finger extension, or abnormal hand postures during particular playing patterns.

Focal dystonia involves neurological factors related to motor control, sensorimotor integration, and plasticity.

Therefore, it should not be reduced to:

> *“The person simply learned to become tense.”*

However, there is a potentially relevant overlap:

**A particular movement representation can become associated with abnormal muscle activity and abnormal motor patterns.**

The important distinction is that the present model describes a potentially shared learning principle; it does not claim that focal dystonia and ordinary tension are the same disorder.

### 8. Yips

Yips are also heterogeneous and may involve combinations of neurological, psychological, attentional, and situational factors.

However, a common feature can be the disruption of an otherwise highly automated skilled movement.

An experienced athlete normally does not consciously control every component of a well-learned movement.

But after a failure or period of pressure, attention may shift toward the movement:

> *“I have to make sure my wrist is positioned correctly.”
“I can’t fail this time.”
“I need to relax my hand.”*

The person begins consciously monitoring something that previously operated automatically.

This can potentially create a loop:

**Excessive monitoring**
↓
**Increased bodily tension**
↓
**Movement disruption**
↓
**Failure or near-failure**
↓
**Increased anticipation of failure**
↓
**More monitoring and tension**

This can make the next attempt even more difficult.

### 9. Everyday Situational Tension

The same mechanism should not be assumed to explain all everyday tension or anxiety.

However, at a more general level, many everyday reactions can be understood through the structure:

**Specific situation or cognition → learned bodily response**

Examples include:

* becoming tense only when speaking in public
* the throat becoming tight during phone calls
* the hands becoming tense during precision work
* the body becoming rigid when interacting with a particular person
* becoming physically tense when something “must not go wrong”
* suddenly losing natural movement in an important situation

These are not necessarily disorders.

They can simply represent learned, context-dependent physiological responses.

### 10. The Reverse-Learning Hypothesis

This is the central idea.

If:

**Specific cognition → tension**

can become reinforced through repetition, then perhaps:

**Specific cognition → relaxation**

can also be learned.

In other words:

**The same principle may work in the opposite direction.**

If repeated pairing of a particular situation with tension gradually makes tension automatic, then repeatedly entering that situation while maintaining a genuinely relaxed bodily state may create a competing learned association.

The goal is not necessarily to “fight” the tension.

It is to provide the nervous system with another possible response.

### 11. Doing the Opposite of What Was Learned

The basic idea can be stated very simply:

> ***If the same thing was learned in one direction, the opposite state can potentially be learned through the same principle.***

For example:

### Original pattern

**Specific situation**
↓
Tension
↓
Muscular contraction
↓
Action

### Alternative pattern

**Specific situation**
↓
Relaxed bodily state
↓
Action without unnecessary contraction

The important point is not a specific number of repetitions.

There is no simple rule such as:

> *“You practiced the old pattern 1,000 times, so you need to practice the new pattern 1,000 times.”*

Neuroplasticity does not work like a simple counter.

What matters is the **quality and state of the learning**.

### 12. Preventing Tension vs. Relaxing After Tension Appears

There is an important difference between:

**Tension → notice it → try to relax**

and:

**Recognize the situation → establish relaxation before the tension response begins → enter the situation in that state**

The second approach is fundamentally different.

It does not necessarily attempt to eliminate an already-established tension response.

Instead, it attempts to establish a different bodily state **before the learned tension response is activated**.

The subjective experience can therefore become:

> *“I know this situation normally makes me tense.”
↓
Establish the relaxed bodily sensation beforehand.
↓
Enter the situation.
↓
The expected tension never appears.*

This is different from successfully suppressing tension after it has already emerged.

### 13. The Importance of Actually Knowing the Sensation of Relaxation

Simply telling someone:

> *“Relax.”*

is not necessarily enough.

The person needs some actual bodily reference for what relaxation feels like.

Once someone can recognize and reproduce a genuinely relaxed state, they gain another option besides the familiar tension response.

Instead of:

> *“I have to stop myself from becoming tense.”*

the process becomes:

> *“I know what a non-tense state feels like, and I can enter this situation from there.”*

This changes the problem from **suppressing a response** to **expanding the available repertoire of bodily states**.

### 14. The User’s Personal Observation

In the present model, an important observation comes from personal experience.

The user has found that when a particular situation is approaching, introducing the sensation of relaxation beforehand can sometimes prevent the expected tension from appearing at all.

This is not experienced as:

> *“I became tense and then forced myself to relax.”*

It is closer to:

> *“I know this state is coming.”
↓
Apply the sensation of relaxation beforehand.
↓
The tension does not arise.*

This distinction is important.

The intervention is not necessarily about suppressing the reaction.

It may instead be changing the bodily state **before the reaction is triggered**.

The user also reports that this has generalized to a very large portion of everyday life rather than remaining limited to one particular movement.

That raises the possibility that the intervention is not merely correcting individual movements but may be changing the broader tendency to automatically generate excessive bodily tension in response to cognition and situations.

This remains an individual observation, however, and should not be generalized into a medical treatment claim.

### 15. “Most People Only Know How to Become Tense”

One particularly interesting part of this model is the possibility that many people simply do not have a practiced alternative.

When people become tense, they may know how to:

* try harder
* endure it
* concentrate more
* use willpower
* tell themselves to calm down
* take a deep breath
* force themselves through the situation

But these strategies do not necessarily amount to having a **well-developed bodily state that is genuinely different from tension**.

As a result, someone may effectively know only one way of operating in a particular situation:

**“This is how my body functions here: tense.”**

The problem is then not necessarily that the person lacks the ability to relax in general.

They may simply lack the learned ability to **remain relaxed while performing that particular action or entering that particular situation**.

### 16. Tension and Relaxation as Learnable States

This does not mean:

**Tension = bad**
**Relaxation = good**

Tension is an essential physiological function.

The problem arises when excessive tension is automatically recruited in situations where it is unnecessary.

Therefore, the ideal outcome is not necessarily to eliminate tension entirely.

It is to increase the ability to choose or access the appropriate bodily state for the situation.

In other words:

**The goal is not permanent relaxation.**

The goal is:

**greater flexibility in the relationship between cognition, situation, movement, and bodily state.**

### 17. Neuroplasticity Is Not Inherently Negative

The deeper principle is:

**Neuroplasticity itself is not the problem.**

What matters is which pattern is repeatedly reinforced.

If a movement is repeatedly performed with excessive tension:

**Movement → tension**

may become increasingly automatic.

But if the same movement is repeatedly performed in a relaxed and efficient state:

**Movement → relaxed execution**

may become increasingly available.

Thus, neuroplasticity is both:

**a mechanism that can reinforce dysfunctional patterns**

and

**a mechanism that can establish alternative patterns.**

The critical question becomes:

> ***What state are you repeatedly practicing?***

### 18. “The Same Amount of Reverse Practice” Is Not a Literal Formula

It would be too simplistic to say:

> *“If the dysfunctional pattern was practiced 1,000 times, practicing the opposite pattern 1,000 times will undo it.”*

Plasticity is influenced by many factors:

* repetition
* attention
* sensory feedback
* physiological state
* movement accuracy
* fatigue
* stress
* emotional significance
* successful experiences
* context
* competition between old and new patterns

Therefore, the more accurate statement is not:

**“Equal repetition cancels previous repetition.”**

It is:

**“The same principles of plasticity can potentially be used in the opposite direction.”**

### 19. Relaxation Alone May Potentially Be Enough to Produce Change

An additional hypothesis follows from the user’s experience.

It may not always be necessary to deliberately retrain every problematic movement.

If the broader tendency toward excessive tension can be reduced, then the person may simply stop repeatedly activating the old tension pattern.

For example:

**Specific cognition**
↓
Normally triggers tension
↓
But tension no longer appears
↓
The person performs the activity normally

Every successful experience becomes another instance in which:

**“This situation does not require tension.”**

The person may therefore be learning a new relationship without explicitly thinking of it as “rehabilitation.”

Simply **living more often in a non-tensed state** may itself alter the balance of learned responses.

This remains a hypothesis rather than an established treatment principle for neurological disorders.

### 20. The Overall Model

The complete model can be summarized as follows.

### Original learning

**Specific situation / cognition**
↓
Prediction of action
↓
Bodily tension
↓
Muscular contraction / physiological arousal
↓
Action performed in that state
↓
Repeated experience
↓
Neuroplasticity
↓
**“This cognition = tension” becomes increasingly automatic**

Eventually:

**The person may become tense before consciously deciding to tense.**

### Reverse learning

**Specific situation / cognition**
↓
Establish relaxation before the tension response begins
↓
Perform the action while maintaining that relaxed state
↓
Experience that the action can succeed without excessive tension
↓
Repeated experience
↓
A competing bodily pattern becomes available
↓
The automatic tension response may weaken

And if this approach generalizes broadly:

**More of everyday life is experienced without unnecessary tension.**

The body may gradually become less dependent on tension as its default response to particular cognitions and situations.

### 21. The Central Idea

The core of this model is not:

> ***“Stop being tense.”***

It is:

> ***“Learn to use the opposite state.”***

And even more precisely:

> ***“Instead of trying not to become tense, experience that the action can be performed while the body remains relaxed.”***

If tension can be learned, relaxation can potentially be learned as well.

If a particular cognition can become associated with tension, another bodily state can potentially become associated with that same cognition.

From this perspective, neuroplasticity is not simply a mechanism that creates bad habits.

It is a mechanism that strengthens **whatever body–cognition pattern is repeatedly experienced**.

The deeper question therefore becomes:

> ***What state is being practiced?***

At the same time, focal dystonia and other neurological movement disorders should not be reduced to this model alone. They involve complex neurological mechanisms and require appropriate medical assessment and treatment. The present framework is best understood as a conceptual model for thinking about the common ground between learned bodily tension, yips, performance-related tension, and everyday conditioned physical responses.
