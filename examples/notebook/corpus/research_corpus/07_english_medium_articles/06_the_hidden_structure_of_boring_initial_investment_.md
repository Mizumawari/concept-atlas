### The Hidden Structure of “Boring Initial Investment” and the Point Where Everyday Life Itself Becomes Practice

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*YZwjr7igV_H1KDdmPJD0gQ.png)

### 1. Starting Point of the Inquiry

This inquiry began with a piece of writing about a common teaching pattern found across three seemingly different fields: hands-on treatment, martial arts, and meditation.

The original writer points out that the teaching approaches they are currently exposed to share a common characteristic:

They initially devote most of their resources to cultivating a kind of felt sense that eventually allows the student to become capable of progressing without continually being taught.

The emphasis is not on teaching a large number of techniques or accumulating knowledge.

Instead, the student is encouraged to concentrate almost entirely, at least initially, on grasping the single most important and highest-priority sense — the thing that cannot be compromised.

Once that core sense is established, the student can supposedly continue improving spontaneously within everyday life.

The original writing also emphasizes temporarily letting go of everything learned in the past.

This description is not my own original wording. It is the starting point for the present analysis.

The questions explored here are:

* Why might this type of teaching work?
* Why is the initial stage so difficult for many people to sustain?
* Why is the initial investment difficult to evaluate rationally in advance?
* Why can everyday life itself eventually become a form of practice?
* Why can people who appear to be doing similar things end up with radically different results?
* And why does this structure seem to connect naturally with religious ideas such as surrender, letting go, and entrusting oneself to something?[https://medium.com/media/e2731096c86a7e7ceffb6ce26c4373aa/href](https://medium.com/media/e2731096c86a7e7ceffb6ce26c4373aa/href)

### 2. What Is Actually Being Taught Is Not Just a Technique

The common feature may not simply be that these teachers are teaching techniques in a particular way.

They may be trying to cultivate something more fundamental:

**an internal criterion by which the student can recognize and correct their own state.**

In ordinary learning, the structure often looks something like:

Teacher gives an instruction.

↓

Student understands it.

↓

Student practices.

↓

Teacher corrects the student.

↓

Student practices again.

In the teaching style described above, the teacher’s role may be somewhat different.

Instead of continuously supplying answers, the teacher helps the student acquire a criterion that allows them to recognize the answer through their own experience.

For example:

In martial arts, knowing the form of a technique is different from being able to feel whether the technique is actually functioning correctly within one’s own body.

In bodywork, knowing which body part to manipulate is different from being able to perceive the body’s actual state.

In meditation, knowing how a particular state is described is different from being able to recognize whether one is moving toward that state or unnecessarily interfering with the process.

Thus, what is being cultivated is not merely technical knowledge.

It is an internal feedback mechanism.

Once that mechanism becomes reliable, the student can continue learning even when the teacher is absent.

### 3. The Core Sense Becomes a Self-Correction Mechanism

The important point is that the core sense is not merely an answer.

It becomes a mechanism for self-correction.

Instead of asking:

> *“What is the correct thing to do?”*

the student gradually becomes able to ask:

> *“What is happening right now, and am I moving toward or away from the essential condition?”*

That is a fundamentally different learning structure.

The student no longer has to memorize every possible correct response.

They can use their own internal feedback to adjust.

This makes it possible for learning to continue outside formal practice sessions.

The teacher’s involvement can therefore decrease over time.

Paradoxically, a good teaching system may be one that makes the teacher progressively less necessary.

### 4. The Problem: The Initial Work Is Extremely Boring

The biggest difficulty with this approach is that the initial process is often extraordinarily mundane.

The actual task may not be complicated.

It may involve repeatedly doing something very simple.

Feel a subtle difference.

Repeat.

Do not understand it.

Repeat again.

Feel that something has changed slightly.

Repeat again.

From the outside, it may look as if almost nothing is happening.

There may be little visible progress.

There may be no obvious reward.

There may not even be a clear explanation of what the eventual result will look like.

And there is another problem:

**Nobody knows in advance how much time will be required.**

Is it 100 hours?

500?

2,000?

5,000?

The answer will vary between individuals, and even the definition of “having acquired it” may not be clear beforehand.

This makes the initial investment extremely difficult to evaluate rationally.

### 5. Why the Initial Investment Is Difficult to Rationalize in Advance

In my own case, I felt that it took roughly 2,000 hours of concentrated repetition before I had developed a sufficiently stable foundation that I was much less likely to misrecognize the core of the practice.

This number should not be interpreted as a universal prescription.

It does not mean that everyone needs 2,000 hours.

It does not mean that 2,000 hours guarantees a particular result.

And I did not begin with a rational calculation that investing 2,000 hours would eventually produce a certain outcome.

That is precisely the important point.

If I had known in advance:

> *“Invest 2,000 hours now, and afterward a large part of your everyday life will itself become practice,”*

then the investment would have been relatively easy to justify.

But before making the investment, none of that was certain.

I could not know:

* whether the method would really work,
* whether it would work for me,
* how much time it would require,
* whether another method might be more efficient,
* when meaningful results would appear,
* or even exactly what “success” would mean.

Therefore, the initial investment has an unusual structure:

**A large amount of time must be invested before the future return can be properly understood.**

### 6. In My Case, I Was Simply Lucky Enough to Feel Some Value in It

I do not think I was naturally the type of person who was especially suited to this kind of learning.

In fact, I tend toward the opposite.

I am inclined to regard my own felt sense as authoritative, and I have historically been extremely prone to doing things my own way.

In that sense, I would not describe myself as someone who naturally excels at unquestioningly following a teaching for a long period of time.

Yet the process happened to work.

I happened to experience something valuable in the practice itself.

That small sense of “this is good” was enough to allow the repetition to continue.

I did not have a clear rational forecast of the eventual outcome.

I did not know that the process would eventually become integrated into everyday life.

I simply had enough immediate experiential value to keep going.

Looking backward, a very large amount of time had accumulated.

So I currently regard myself less as someone who succeeded because of exceptional discipline or talent and more as someone who was **lucky enough for the conditions necessary for sustained practice to emerge.**

### 7. Why Many People Cannot Continue the Boring Work

It is tempting to explain the inability to continue such work as a lack of willpower.

But there may be a more structural explanation.

Most people have some degree of spare capacity.

They can continue living with their existing methods.

They can try something else.

They can acquire new information.

They can change approaches.

They can stop if the results do not seem convincing.

In other words:

**They have enough room to remain on the trajectory they were already following.**

That makes it difficult to justify fixing a large amount of resources onto one extremely narrow and apparently unrewarding task.

If the task does not immediately make sense, they can switch.

If another approach seems more efficient, they can try it.

If they become uncertain, they can return to what they already know.

This means that the problem may not simply be an inability to work hard.

It may be that there is no compelling reason to leave the existing trajectory.

### 8. Having Spare Capacity May Actually Make This Harder

This creates an apparent paradox.

Having more resources and more options is normally considered advantageous.

But when the goal is to develop an unfamiliar internal sense through long-term repetition, having many options may make the process harder.

If alternatives are always available, one can:

* try another method,
* return to an old method,
* modify the practice according to one’s own ideas,
* stop when progress is unclear,
* or constantly reevaluate whether the current approach is worthwhile.

Each of these choices may be reasonable.

But together, they prevent resources from becoming concentrated in one place for a sufficiently long period.

By contrast, someone who has no alternative may simply continue.

They may not possess greater willpower.

They may simply have fewer opportunities to distribute their resources elsewhere.

In this sense, **“having no choice but to continue” can itself create a powerful learning condition.**

### 9. Possible Types of People Who Can Sustain the Initial Investment

Several types of people may be able to cross this initial barrier.

### 1. The lucky person

The necessary conditions happen to arise.

There is enough time.

The environment supports repetition.

The person happens to experience some immediate value.

Daily life allows the practice to continue.

No exceptional talent is necessarily required.

### 2. The person with an existing foundation

Someone has already been practicing continuously for a long time.

They already possess the habit and volume of practice.

However, the core element has not yet been fully refined.

In this case, the existing foundation can be redirected toward the essential point.

### 3. The person who can accept it without hesitation

Someone may have been close to people who already embody the ability.

Because of that direct exposure, they may be able to accept the instruction without endlessly questioning it.

Or they may simply possess a disposition that allows them to commit without much hesitation.

The important characteristic here may not be “believing the teacher is right.”

It may simply be the ability to avoid continually reopening the decision.

### 4. The person who has no other choice

Someone may be in a situation where abandoning the process is not a realistic option.

Again, this does not necessarily mean greater strength of character.

The environment itself removes alternatives.

As a result, resources remain concentrated.

### 10. What These Types Have in Common: Leaving the Existing Trajectory

Although these people arrive at the process through different routes, they share something important.

They somehow manage to move outside the trajectory established by their previous habits.

This does not necessarily mean making a dramatic life change.

It can be much smaller.

It means temporarily suspending the usual way of deciding:

> *“I already know how I would normally do this.”*

Instead:

> *“For now, I will follow this one criterion and see what happens.”*

That small deviation from the familiar trajectory can be surprisingly difficult.

The old trajectory has a reason for existing.

It has worked well enough to sustain one’s life.

It contains previous successes.

It contains accumulated knowledge.

Therefore, abandoning it requires giving up something that is already familiar and functional in exchange for something whose value is not yet clear.

### 11. Temporarily Letting Go of Previous Learning

This connects with the original statement that previous learning should temporarily be let go.

The problem with previous learning is not necessarily that it is wrong.

The problem is that it can become an overly strong interpretive framework.

A new bodily experience appears.

The mind immediately tries to classify it using existing knowledge.

“This must be the same thing I learned before.”

“This probably means I should activate this muscle.”

“This is probably the correct way to do it.”

The result is that the person may be trying to reproduce an interpretation rather than actually perceiving the new experience.

Temporarily letting go of previous learning can therefore be understood as:

**moving previous knowledge out of the position of absolute authority.**

It does not mean destroying or permanently rejecting that knowledge.

It means allowing direct experience to come first, with conceptual knowledge available afterward as a reference.

### 12. When Everyday Life Itself Becomes Practice

Initially, there is a clear distinction:

**Practice time = learning**

**Ordinary life = everything else**

But once the core sense becomes sufficiently stable, this distinction begins to dissolve.

Walking.

Sitting.

Standing.

Working.

Resting.

Using the body.

Doing ordinary daily activities.

All of these can become opportunities to receive feedback.

The person no longer needs to create a special practice environment every time.

The important change is therefore not simply:

**“Practice time increases.”**

It is:

**“The range of time that can function as practice expands.”**

Eventually, everyday life itself becomes a continuous feedback environment.

### 13. The Structural Change Around the 2,000-Hour Point

In my own experience, after roughly 2,000 hours of concentrated repetition, I began to feel that I had developed a foundation that made it much harder to fundamentally misrecognize the core of the practice.

Again, the number itself is not the important part.

The important part is what happened to the structure of time afterward.

Before the foundation was established:

**Practice time = practice**

**Everyday life = ordinary life**

Afterward:

**Practice continues during ordinary life.**

The distinction becomes increasingly blurred.

Walking, sitting, working, resting, and moving through daily activities can all provide ongoing feedback.

Therefore, the actual amount of committed time is much larger than the initial 2,000 hours.

The initial investment creates the conditions under which everyday life itself can become a continuing process of repetition and self-correction.

### 14. The Difference Between Repeating an Action and Learning Through It

Two people can perform exactly the same external behavior every day.

Both may walk every day.

Both may train every day.

Both may practice a particular posture.

But the internal learning process can be completely different.

For one person, walking is simply transportation.

For another, walking is also an opportunity to detect subtle bodily deviations and automatically adjust them.

From the outside, the behavior looks almost identical.

Internally, however, one person is simply repeating an action while the other is continuously receiving feedback from it.

Therefore:

**External repetition is not necessarily equivalent to learning.**

What matters is whether the repetition contains a functioning feedback loop.

Once the core sense is sufficiently established, ordinary activities can become part of that loop.

### 15. Why Similar-Looking Practice Can Produce Radically Different Results

This provides one possible explanation for a familiar phenomenon:

**Two people appear to be doing almost the same thing, yet their results are completely different.**

Both practice every day.

Both spend time using their bodies.

Both appear to follow the same method.

But one person may simply be repeating the external form.

The other may be continuously checking and adjusting themselves through an internal criterion.

The difference is almost invisible from the outside.

Over years, however, the accumulated difference can become enormous.

This suggests that measuring practice only by visible hours may be inadequate.

A more meaningful question may be:

**How much of a person’s daily life is actually functioning as learning?**

### 16. The Appropriate Amount of Time Cannot Be Known in Advance

Another important feature of this type of learning is that the appropriate amount of time is difficult to determine beforehand.

People differ in:

* prior foundation,
* environment,
* sensitivity to bodily feedback,
* ability to maintain attention,
* accumulated practice,
* and the point at which they consider the ability sufficiently established.

Therefore, a simple rule such as:

> *“Practice for X hours and you will acquire it”*

is unlikely to capture the actual process.

Instead, the process may look more like:

Practice a little.

↓

Notice some value.

↓

Continue.

↓

Begin to recognize something.

↓

Continue.

↓

Eventually notice:

> *“Wait — something has changed.”*

The future result does not necessarily need to be understood in advance.

A small present-moment reward can be enough to sustain the next stage.

### 17. The “Wait, What?” Moment

That moment of:

**“Wait, something is different.”**

may be particularly important.

At first, the person may simply experience something as slightly better.

Then gradually:

* something becomes more natural,
* a distinction becomes easier to perceive,
* a correction happens without deliberate effort,
* the same sense appears during ordinary life,
* or something that previously required conscious attention begins happening automatically.

Only afterward does the meaning of the earlier boring work become clear.

In this sense:

**The investment is not rationalized first and rewarded later.**

Instead:

**The result eventually makes the earlier investment understandable.**

This is one reason why rational cost-benefit analysis has difficulty evaluating certain forms of learning in advance.

### 18. The Connection to Surrender, Letting Go, and Entrusting

This structure also helps explain why such practices can easily become connected with religious concepts such as surrender, letting go, or entrusting oneself to something.

At the beginning:

* the required amount of time is unknown,
* the future result is unknown,
* the mechanism is not completely understood,
* and the person cannot fully evaluate whether the process will work.

Yet they continue.

In religious language, this can easily be described as:

**surrender, trust, letting go, or entrusting oneself.**

However, “letting go” here does not necessarily mean abandoning judgment or becoming passive.

It can instead mean:

**stopping the attempt to repeatedly evaluate and manipulate something that one is not yet capable of evaluating accurately.**

### 19. Letting Go Does Not Mean Doing Nothing

The phrase “letting go” can sound like giving up effort.

But within this framework, the opposite may be closer to the truth.

Letting go means reducing unnecessary intervention so that the necessary process can continue.

Constantly asking:

* “Is this really worthwhile?”
* “Is there a more efficient method?”
* “Am I doing it correctly?”
* “How many hours should I continue?”
* “When will I get results?”

consumes cognitive resources.

Those resources could instead be invested in actually performing and sensing the practice.

Thus:

**Letting go does not mean doing nothing.**

It can mean:

**reducing unnecessary manipulation and allowing the necessary repetition to continue.**

### 20. Surrender Does Not Necessarily Lead to Permanent Dependence

There is another interesting possibility.

Surrender does not necessarily have to mean remaining dependent on an external authority forever.

At first:

> *“I cannot yet recognize this myself, so I will trust the teacher or the method.”*

Later, after sufficient repetition:

> *“I can now recognize this directly myself.”*

At that point, dependence on the teacher can decrease.

The progression can therefore look like:

**Trust in another person**

↓

**Trust in the method**

↓

**Development of direct bodily recognition**

↓

**Trust in one’s own internal feedback**

↓

**Autonomy**

From this perspective, teaching someone how to become independent and temporarily entrusting oneself to a teacher are not necessarily contradictory.

Temporary surrender may actually function as a bridge toward autonomy.

### 21. Why Ordinary People May Remain on the Existing Trajectory

This leads back to the question of why people generally have difficulty leaving their existing trajectory.

It may not be because they lack discipline.

It may be because they have enough options.

They can continue living normally.

They can try something else.

They can stop.

They can return to familiar methods.

They can preserve their existing worldview.

There is no immediate necessity to abandon it.

Therefore:

**The very fact that alternatives are available can make deep commitment to an uncertain path less likely.**

By contrast, when a person has:

* no meaningful alternative,
* unusually strong immediate experiential feedback,
* an existing foundation,
* or unusually low hesitation,

the same long-term commitment can become possible.

This means that people who eventually make a dramatic transition may not have had a clearer vision of the future.

They may simply have continued following a small present-moment signal long enough for the future structure to reveal itself.

### 22. The Overall Structure

Putting the entire argument together:

A person encounters a core sense that appears to be fundamental.

The task of developing that sense is extremely simple but extremely boring.

The necessary investment is unknown.

The future return is unknown.

Therefore, the investment is difficult to rationally justify in advance.

Most people can remain on their existing trajectory because they have alternatives and sufficient room to do so.

Some people, however, manage to concentrate their resources on the one essential point.

The reason may be luck, an existing foundation, lack of hesitation, direct exposure to someone who already embodies the skill, or simply having no other choice.

Through repeated exposure, the person gradually becomes able to recognize the core sense accurately.

Once that happens, the person no longer needs to learn only during formal practice.

Everyday life itself becomes a source of feedback.

Walking, sitting, standing, working, resting, and moving through ordinary life all become opportunities for repetition and self-correction.

At that point:

**Practice becomes integrated into life.**

The distinction between “practice time” and “ordinary time” becomes increasingly weak.

As a result, the person’s actual cumulative learning time may become enormous even though their visible lifestyle appears completely ordinary.

### 23. The Central Question Is Not Talent or Effort Alone

The central issue may therefore not simply be:

**How talented is the person?**

or:

**How hard does the person work?**

A more fundamental question is:

**Can the person maintain a sufficiently large investment in a point whose value they cannot yet fully understand?**

And then:

**Can that point eventually become an internal criterion that functions automatically throughout everyday life?**

The initial investment is difficult precisely because its future value cannot be properly calculated beforehand.

Therefore, the conditions that allow the investment to continue may be more important than raw willpower.

In my own case, the important conditions were that I happened to experience some value in the practice and that I happened to be able to cultivate it continuously, repetitively, and for long periods within everyday life.

After roughly 2,000 hours, I felt that I had developed a sufficiently stable foundation that I was much less likely to fundamentally misrecognize the core of the practice.

From there, a substantial portion of everyday life itself began functioning as practice.

Thus, the actual commitment cannot be represented by “2,000 hours” alone.

The larger effect came from the fact that those initial 2,000 hours changed the structure of subsequent time.

### 24. From “Practicing” to “Living the Practice”

Ultimately, the goal may not be to become someone who knows a large number of techniques.

Nor is it necessarily to become someone who continually receives correct answers from a teacher.

It may instead be to become someone who can:

**receive feedback from their own body, recognize what is happening, correct themselves, and continue learning without external instruction.**

That requires a core sense.

Developing that sense may require temporarily suspending previous learning and habitual self-judgment.

It may require a long period of boring repetition whose eventual value cannot be predicted.

And if that process succeeds, everyday life itself can become practice.

The progression can therefore be described as:

**“Practicing”**

↓

**“Living while practicing”**

↓

**“Life itself becoming practice.”**

At that point, the person may no longer need to think of themselves as “doing practice.”

They are simply living.

But their ordinary life continues to provide the repetition, feedback, and self-correction that once required deliberate practice sessions.

### 25. Final Synthesis

The deepest point of this inquiry may be that the decisive factor in development is not simply talent or effort.

It may be the ability to make a sufficiently large and sufficiently long investment in a very small, very boring, initially uncertain point.

The initial investment is difficult because its value cannot be calculated in advance.

That is why people naturally tend to remain on the trajectory they already understand.

They have alternatives.

They have spare capacity.

They have previous knowledge.

They have familiar methods.

They can keep evaluating.

They can keep modifying.

They can keep searching.

But all of those possibilities can also prevent sufficient concentration.

The person who somehow leaves that trajectory can accumulate a different kind of learning.

The core sense becomes more stable.

The internal feedback mechanism becomes more reliable.

The need for external instruction decreases.

And eventually, the distinction between practice and ordinary life begins to disappear.

The most interesting transformation is therefore not simply:

**“I became better at practicing.”**

It is:

**“The conditions that once required deliberate practice became embedded in ordinary life.”**

The initial investment creates the foundation.

The foundation turns everyday life into feedback.

The feedback turns everyday life into continued practice.

Continued practice produces greater autonomy.

And autonomy eventually makes the original teacher increasingly unnecessary.

This may also explain why concepts such as surrender, letting go, and entrusting oneself appear so naturally in religious and contemplative traditions.

At the beginning, one may have to temporarily relinquish the demand to understand everything in advance.

But the purpose of that surrender does not necessarily have to be permanent dependence.

It can be the temporary suspension of excessive self-interference until direct internal recognition becomes possible.

The trajectory can therefore be understood as:

**External guidance → concentrated repetition → internal recognition → everyday feedback → autonomous learning.**

Or, in more experiential terms:

**“Being taught” → “practicing” → “recognizing” → “living” → “no longer needing to be taught.”**

The deepest investment may therefore not be the accumulation of techniques.

It may be the cultivation of a single internal criterion that is reliable enough to turn ordinary life itself into a continuous learning environment.
