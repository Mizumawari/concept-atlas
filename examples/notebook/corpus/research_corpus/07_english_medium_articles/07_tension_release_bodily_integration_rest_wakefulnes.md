### Tension Release, Bodily Integration, Rest, Wakefulness, and Sleep — Can Deep Relaxation and High Activation Coexist?

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*AwTI0wPjHmS_J63AZKih6Q.png)

### 0. Position of This Exploration

This document integrates a series of first-person observations concerning:

* deliberately releasing bodily and mental tension
* internal bodily integration
* deep relaxation
* wakefulness and subjective activation
* cognitive clarity
* physical activity
* sleep
* and the possibility of reducing sleep requirements

The terms used here — “activation,” “ease,” “mental clarity,” “bodily integration,” “qi-like sensation,” and “maximum relaxation” — refer to subjective and embodied experiences.

They should therefore be treated as a phenomenological model for further observation, not as proof of any particular physiological mechanism.
[https://medium.com/media/3c52fe599f2632cc88f4a11e0c1c9275/href](https://medium.com/media/3c52fe599f2632cc88f4a11e0c1c9275/href)

### 1. The Starting Point: Questioning the Simple Model of Relaxation

A common intuitive model is:

> *Release tension
→ relax
→ parasympathetic/rest state
→ sleepiness*

The person initially experienced something close to this.

When deliberately relaxing the body — sometimes simply allowing it to become limp, and sometimes intensifying relaxation during exhalation — the experience could become:

* strongly restful
* increasingly parasympathetic-like
* increasingly detached from active wakefulness
* almost as if the support structure of consciousness were disappearing
* close to falling asleep

In this mode, relaxation and sleepiness were closely coupled.

Thus, “releasing tension” initially functioned primarily as a tool for resting or falling asleep.

However, later observations revealed that this was only one possible outcome of the same general operation.

### 2. “Releasing Tension” Is Not a Single State

The person can now deliberately perform an internal operation that feels like “releasing tension” more or less at will.

However, there appear to be several distinct forms of this operation.

At least two can be distinguished.

### A. Maximum Relaxation / Sleep-Oriented State

Characteristics include:

* allowing the body to become very loose
* relaxation becoming stronger during exhalation
* a strong sense of rest
* a feeling that the support structure of consciousness is disappearing
* approaching the point of falling asleep
* potentially producing a feeling that could be frightening for some people because consciousness seems to be losing its foothold

This was historically used mainly for:

> *resting or falling asleep.*

It is therefore closely associated with the sleep transition.

### B. Bodily Integration / Deep Structural Tension

There is another state in which superficial tension is extremely low, while the body nevertheless feels internally organized and structurally integrated.

Characteristics include:

* breathing becoming almost motionless
* little or no deliberate respiratory manipulation
* very little superficial tension
* a sense that the body is standing or organizing itself beautifully through deeper layers
* a strong sense of internal bodily integration
* a sensation somewhat resembling “qi”
* the sensation becoming somewhat stronger during exhalation
* stronger relaxation tending to intensify the sensation

This is not the same as simply becoming limp.

Rather:

> *unnecessary superficial tension is released, while deeper structural organization remains intact.*

The body is not collapsing into rest.

It is highly integrated while remaining relaxed.

### 3. These Two States Had Not Previously Been Combined Very Much

Until recently, the person had generally used these two processes separately.

In particular:

* breathing was rarely used consciously anymore
* when it was used, it was usually very weak
* maximum relaxation was mainly used for sleep or rest
* there had not been a strong regular exercise habit
* there was little intention to combine maximum relaxation with bodily integration

Thus, the two processes existed independently:

> *bodily integration*

and

> *maximum relaxation.*

But the possibility of combining them had not been explored very much.

The recent discovery may therefore have resulted from combining:

> ***Bodily integration + maximum relaxation.***

### 4. Maximum Relaxation Alone Tends Toward Sleep

When the maximum-relaxation state is used by itself, the trajectory tends to be:

> *release tension
→ deeper relaxation
→ loss of the normal foothold of consciousness
→ drowsiness / drifting toward sleep*

This is consistent with the person’s previous experience.

Therefore, the earlier understanding that:

> *“Maximum relaxation makes me sleepy”*

was not wrong.

It may simply have described only one configuration of relaxation.

### 5. Bodily Integration Alone Does Not Necessarily Mean Deep Rest

The bodily-integration state is different.

It involves:

* clean structural organization
* deeper internal tension
* a sense of bodily unity
* a qi-like sensation

But it does not necessarily mean maximum rest.

Maintaining structural integration and entering a maximally restful state may be separate processes.

### 6. The Central Discovery: Bodily Integration + Maximum Relaxation

The new possibility is:

> *Establish bodily integration
+
layer maximum relaxation over it*

In other words:

> *the body remains deeply integrated at a structural level
+
superficial and conscious tension becomes extremely low
+
breathing becomes almost motionless
+
relaxation becomes deep enough that even the normal foothold of consciousness begins to disappear*

This combination appears capable of producing an unusually high degree of mental clarity.

That outcome had not previously been expected.

### 7. The Apparent Paradox: Maximum Relaxation and Maximum Vitality

This is the most important observation.

The intuitive model is:

> *deeper relaxation
→ lower activation
→ sleepiness*

But the current experience suggests another possibility:

> *deep relaxation
+
bodily integration
→ very high subjective activation
+
extreme physical ease
+
exceptionally clear cognition
+
intense cerebral wakefulness*

In other words:

> ***Deep relaxation and high activation may not necessarily be opposites.***

The “activation” described here should not be interpreted simply as nervous excitation or heightened tension.

It is closer to:

* the body feeling extremely easy to move
* very little unnecessary effort
* high physical vitality
* extremely clear cognition
* a strong sense of being fully awake

The state is therefore better described as:

> ***high vitality without unnecessary tension***

rather than simply “high arousal.”

### 8. Mental Clarity Appears to Have Levels

The person does not experience “mental clarity” as a single binary state.

As a subjective scale:

* previous ordinary state: roughly 30–60
* current ordinary baseline: consistently above roughly 70
* the best-feeling awakening ever experienced: above roughly 90
* the newly observed state: potentially beyond that 90+ level

These numbers are not physiological measurements.

They are simply a subjective framework for comparing different levels of clarity.

This distinction matters because the person’s current baseline is already unusually clear.

Therefore, the new state is not merely:

> *“I am not sleepy.”*

It may represent:

> ***a further increase in clarity above an already high baseline.***

For someone starting from a much lower baseline, the same transition might be experienced as an extraordinarily intense awakening.

### 9. Cognitive Clarity vs. Felt Cerebral Clarity

The current experience seems to contain at least two different forms of “clarity.”

### Cognitive / Perceptual Clarity

This includes:

* clear perception
* low cognitive noise
* clear thinking
* stable attention
* clean processing
* absence of mental fog

### Felt Cerebral Clarity

This is more directly phenomenological.

It resembles:

> *“The brain itself feels completely awake.”*

Characteristics include:

* a vivid sense of being fully awake
* something resembling awakening from extremely deep sleep
* no desire for a second sleep
* a feeling that the brain itself is exceptionally clear
* intense cerebral vitality

The newly observed state seems to combine:

> ***cognitive clarity + felt cerebral clarity.***

### 10. Why “Complete Relaxation” Is a Misleading Term

The phrase “complete relaxation” can easily cause misunderstanding.

It may imply:

* every muscle becoming completely limp
* the body collapsing
* no structural tension
* inactivity

But that is not what is being described.

There is still deep internal organization and structural tension.

A more accurate description would be:

> ***Removing unnecessary tension while preserving deep bodily integration.***

Or:

> ***Maintaining structural bodily integration while reducing superficial and conscious tension as far as possible.***

The important distinction is:

> *relaxed does not necessarily mean structurally collapsed.*

### 11. The Role of Physical Activity

The discovery became particularly clear after substantial physical activity.

The person normally spends a significant amount of time in relatively low-activity conditions, so the relationship between activity and this state had not been obvious.

Then, after spending many minutes performing physically demanding activity outdoors under intense summer heat — something that would generally be considered exhausting — the person became much more aware of the state.

Afterward, instead of simply feeling more fatigued, something unexpected occurred.

The person experienced:

* greater physical ease
* greater bodily activation
* easier movement
* stronger cerebral vitality
* greater overall subjective comfort

Most strikingly:

> ***the body felt even easier than it had when simply resting without exercise.***

This was not merely a reduction in perceived fatigue.

It felt like a transition into a qualitatively better state.

### 12. Activity May Not Simply “Create Fatigue”

The ordinary model is:

> *activity
→ fatigue
→ rest
→ recovery*

The observed experience suggests another possible sequence:

> *physical activity
→ change in bodily state
→ bodily integration
→ maximum relaxation
→ increased physical ease + activation + cerebral clarity*

This creates a possible phenomenological connection with the idea expressed by Daisuke Hori, known for advocating approaches to short sleep, that physical activity can contribute to removing fatigue.

The present observation does not establish that his broader theory is physiologically correct.

Nor does it mean that intense activity — especially in extreme heat — should be used as a fatigue-recovery method.

A more cautious interpretation is:

> ***Physical activity may alter the body’s state in a way that makes a particular deeply relaxed, highly integrated state easier to enter afterward.***

### 13. Physical Fatigue and Low Activation May Be Different Phenomena

The word “fatigue” may conceal several different experiences.

These could include:

* actual physiological fatigue from activity
* low activation from prolonged inactivity
* bodily sluggishness
* poor movement integration
* accumulated physiological fatigue
* subjective heaviness
* mental dullness

All of these may sometimes be described simply as:

> *“I feel tired.”*

The current experience raises the possibility that activity did not literally “remove fatigue.”

Instead:

> *activity may have shifted the body away from a low-activation or stagnant state and made another state accessible.*

Distinguishing these possibilities will be important in future observation.

### 14. The Important Fact: The High-Activation State Does Not Prevent Sleep

The newly observed state is not simply a state of excessive arousal that makes sleep impossible.

The person can still sleep from this state.

That means:

> *extremely high subjective activation
+
deep relaxation
+
strong physical ease
+
ability to fall asleep*

can coexist.

This is important because it suggests:

> ***wakefulness and sleepability may not be simple opposites.***

The person does not have to become dull or exhausted in order to sleep.

The system appears capable of transitioning into sleep even from a state that subjectively feels extremely awake.

### 15. An Even Stranger Observation: Sleep Can Lower the State

Another important observation is that after sleeping, the subjective level of activation and ease is actually somewhat lower than it was in the unusual pre-sleep state.

The sequence is therefore not simply:

> *high activation
→ sleep
→ recovery
→ even higher activation*

Instead:

> *bodily integration + maximum relaxation
→ extremely high activation and ease
→ sleep
→ awakening
→ slightly lower subjective activation/ease*

This does **not** imply that sleep is unnecessary or that sleep failed to provide physiological recovery.

Sleep has functions that cannot be inferred from subjective activation alone.

However, it does challenge the assumption that:

> *“the state after sleep must be the maximum state of recovery.”*

At least on the subjective dimensions of activation and ease, that does not necessarily appear to be true.

### 16. This Leads to a Deeper Question: What Is Sleep?

At this point, the question changes.

It is no longer simply:

> *“How can I sleep less?”*

It becomes:

> ***“What exactly is sleep doing?”***

If a person can simultaneously experience:

* deep relaxation
* bodily integration
* high subjective activation
* high physical ease
* extreme cognitive clarity
* strong cerebral wakefulness
* and the ability to fall asleep normally

then the following simple model becomes insufficient:

> *wakefulness = activity
relaxation = stopping activity
sleep = the ultimate form of relaxation*

A different possibility is:

> ***Sleep is a distinct physiological state with functions of its own, while certain forms of restoration can occur during wakefulness.***

### 17. Rather Than “Replacing Sleep,” Decompose the Functions of Sleep

It may be tempting to ask:

> *“Can this state replace sleep?”*

But a more useful question is:

> ***“Which functions of sleep, if any, can be fulfilled while remaining awake in a deeply restful state?”***

Sleep involves multiple processes, including:

* sleep homeostasis
* memory consolidation
* neural regulation
* metabolic processes
* endocrine regulation
* immune regulation

Therefore, extreme subjective clarity and physical ease do not demonstrate that sleep has become unnecessary.

At the same time, if a deeply relaxed waking state can provide some functions normally associated with recovery, that would be important for understanding the relationship between wakefulness and sleep.

### 18. Reduced Sleepiness Is Not the Same as Reduced Sleep Requirement

This distinction is essential when discussing short sleep.

There is a difference between:

> *“I do not feel sleepy.”*

and:

> *“My biological requirement for sleep has actually decreased.”*

The current experience cannot establish the latter.

However, if the deeply relaxed/high-vitality state could eventually be maintained for long periods without producing negative consequences, while actual sleep duration naturally decreased and subsequent physical and cognitive functioning remained stable, then the relationship with sleep requirement would become a meaningful question.

For now, the appropriate approach is observation rather than deliberately restricting sleep.

### 19. Current Working Model

The observations can tentatively be organized into four states.

### State A — Maximum Relaxation Alone

> *release tension
→ deep relaxation
→ loss of the normal foothold of consciousness
→ drowsiness / drifting toward sleep*

### State B — Bodily Integration

> *release superficial tension
→ deeper structural integration remains
→ body organizes itself efficiently
→ internal integrated sensation / “qi-like” sensation*

### State C — Bodily Integration + Maximum Relaxation

> *establish bodily integration
+
add maximum relaxation
↓
extreme physical ease
+
high physical activation
+
cognitive clarity
+
felt cerebral clarity
+
ability to sleep if desired*

### State D — Activity → Integration + Maximum Relaxation

> *physical activity
→ change in bodily state
→ bodily integration
→ maximum relaxation
→ State C becomes especially pronounced*

In State D, the person experienced greater subjective ease than during ordinary passive rest.

### 20. Why This Was Not Discovered Earlier

Several factors may explain why this combination was not noticed sooner.

### 1. Maximum relaxation was primarily used for sleep

It was treated as:

> *“the state used to fall asleep.”*

Therefore, its potential relationship with wakefulness was not explored.

### 2. Conscious breathing had become minimal

The person had largely stopped deliberately manipulating breathing, using it only weakly when it occurred at all.

This reduced opportunities to intentionally access the strongest form of relaxation associated with exhalation.

### 3. There was no strong exercise habit

The conditions under which activity followed by bodily integration and maximum relaxation could be observed were relatively uncommon.

### 4. There was little intention to combine the two processes

The two states were already familiar individually.

But the question:

> *“What happens if bodily integration is maintained while maximum relaxation is added?”*

had not been actively pursued.

### 21. A Conceptual Shift

The observations suggest reconsidering several assumptions.

### Assumption 1

“More relaxation necessarily means lower activation.”

→ Not necessarily.

### Assumption 2

“High wakefulness requires high bodily tension.”

→ High wakefulness and high tension may be separable.

### Assumption 3

“Rest means stopping activity.”

→ Activity may sometimes facilitate access to a state that feels more restorative than passive inactivity.

### Assumption 4

“If someone is relaxed enough to sleep, their activation must be low.”

→ Very high subjective activation may coexist with the ability to fall asleep.

### Assumption 5

“The state after sleep is necessarily the highest state of recovery.”

→ At least on subjective dimensions such as activation and ease, this does not necessarily appear to be true.

### 22. Future Observation

Rather than immediately attempting to reduce sleep duration, the first step is to characterize the states themselves.

Important variables include:

1. What happens with maximum relaxation alone?
2. What happens with bodily integration alone?
3. What happens when both are established simultaneously?
4. How does the state differ before and after physical activity?
5. Does the amount of activity matter?
6. Does the type of activity matter?
7. How long can the high-vitality state be maintained?
8. How easily can sleep be initiated from that state?
9. How does subjective activation change after sleep?
10. What happens to physical condition the following day?
11. What happens to cognitive performance?
12. Does actual sleep duration naturally change over time?
13. Does subjective clarity correspond to objective performance?
14. Does the state affect physiological markers of recovery or sleep pressure?

It is particularly important not to collapse the following into one variable:

> *absence of sleepiness
physical ease
high activation
cognitive clarity
cerebral vitality*

They should be observed separately.

### 23. Central Working Hypothesis

The most important hypothesis at present is:

> ***Wakefulness and relaxation may not be simple opposites.***

More specifically:

> ***It may be possible to reduce superficial, unnecessary tension while preserving deep bodily integration, allowing the body to enter a deeply restful state without losing high subjective activation and cognitive clarity.***

Furthermore:

> ***Physical activity may not merely increase fatigue; under certain conditions, it may alter the bodily state in a way that facilitates entry into this deeply relaxed, highly integrated state afterward.***

And finally:

> ***Sleep may not simply be “the opposite of wakefulness” or the final stage of relaxation. It may be a distinct physiological state with specific functions, while certain forms of restoration can occur during wakefulness.***

### 24. The Deeper Question

The ultimate question is no longer simply:

> *“Can I sleep less?”*

It is:

> ***How far can human beings simultaneously maintain deep rest and high wakefulness?***

And beyond that:

> ***Is sleep necessary primarily because the body becomes fatigued, or because sleep performs specific physiological processes that cannot be fully reproduced during wakefulness?***

If some functions normally associated with recovery can occur during a deeply relaxed waking state, then short sleep should not be conceptualized merely as:

> *“reducing the amount of sleep.”*

Instead, it could become a question of:

> ***reconstructing the relationship between rest, wakefulness, and sleep.***

However, none of the present observations establish that sleep can be replaced or that sleep requirements have actually been reduced.

The more productive next step is to investigate the relationships among:

> *maximum relaxation
bodily integration
high activation
subjective ease
cognitive clarity
felt cerebral vitality
physical activity
and sleep requirement*

as separate variables.

The broader possibility emerging from these observations is that what is ordinarily grouped together as “being awake,” “resting,” “being active,” and “sleeping” may actually consist of several partially independent dimensions.

The discovery is therefore not simply that “relaxation can make the brain feel more awake.”

It is that:

> ***the relationship between relaxation, activation, bodily integration, recovery, and sleep may be far less linear than ordinary experience suggests.***
