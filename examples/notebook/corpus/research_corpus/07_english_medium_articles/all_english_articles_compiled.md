# How to Relax the Body-The Capillary Sponge Model and Pulse-Synchronized Relaxation
* **File**: 01_how_to_relax_the_body_the_capillary_sponge_model_a.md | **Date**: Fri, 02 Oct 2026 15:28:07 GMT
* **URL**: [https://medium.com/@sato-kazuki/how-to-relax-the-body-the-capillary-sponge-model-and-pulse-synchronized-relaxation-264553a88d4b?source=rss-c95e8770f8e0------2](https://medium.com/@sato-kazuki/how-to-relax-the-body-the-capillary-sponge-model-and-pulse-synchronized-relaxation-264553a88d4b?source=rss-c95e8770f8e0------2)

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*6mMk6k474aDjkhPfK_9qrQ.png)

### 0. About This Document

This document organizes a personal body-sensation model developed through long-term observation of how bodily tension, relaxation, posture, breathing, and internal sensations interact.

The word “relaxation” here does **not** simply mean muscle relaxation or becoming physically limp.

Instead, it refers to a state in which:

* unnecessary tension is reduced,
* deeper tissues regain an appropriate sense of cohesion,
* the body’s natural rhythmic fluctuations can propagate through the tissues,
* and the body does not need to be forcibly manipulated through breathing or conscious muscular control.

The concepts of a “capillary sponge,” “internal flow,” and “pulse synchronization” are metaphors and hypotheses used to describe subjective bodily experience. They should not be interpreted as literal descriptions of human physiology.
[https://medium.com/media/1877bb3e1aae83715e7e0e128d3300c0/href](https://medium.com/media/1877bb3e1aae83715e7e0e128d3300c0/href)

### 1. The Basic Idea

The human body can be imagined, phenomenologically, as a kind of **living three-dimensional sponge permeated by an extensive network of capillaries**.

Rather than viewing the body simply as a collection of muscles and bones, this model emphasizes the complex three-dimensional relationship between:

* muscles,
* connective tissues,
* blood vessels,
* tissue fluids,
* nerves,
* fascia,
* and subtle mechanical movements.

From this perspective, bodily state is not simply a binary distinction between:

> *tense vs. relaxed*

A more useful question is:

> ***Is the tissue in a state where internal movement and flow can occur naturally?***

### 2. Relaxation Is Not the Same as Letting Go

Ordinary descriptions of relaxation often imply:

* releasing muscle tension,
* dropping the shoulders,
* becoming softer,
* breathing deeply,
* or becoming generally relaxed.

But this model suggests that these are not necessarily the same thing.

The body can potentially exist in several different states.

### A. Excessive compression

The tissue is overly contracted or compressed.

This can be imagined as squeezing a sponge tightly.

The internal spaces become compressed, and the subjective sensation may be one of restricted movement or stagnation.

### B. Excessive expansion

The opposite can also occur.

If the body is intentionally expanded or excessively “opened,” the tissue may lose its sense of cohesion.

It can feel relaxed, but without a stable center or internal organization.

### C. Appropriate cohesion

The most important state is somewhere between these extremes.

The body is:

* not hard,
* not rigid,
* not collapsed,
* not excessively expanded,

but gently and naturally organized.

This can be described as:

> ***a sponge returning to an appropriate density.***

### 3. The Core of the Sponge Model

A sponge is not better simply because it is softer.

Nor is it better because it is more expanded.

What matters is whether its internal structure allows fluid and deformation to move through it naturally.

The same conceptual distinction is applied to the body.

The goal is therefore not:

> *“Make the body as relaxed as possible.”*

Instead:

> ***Reduce the factors that interfere with the body’s natural internal movement.***

These factors may include:

* excessive muscular tension,
* local fixation,
* unnecessary postural control,
* excessive breathing manipulation,
* intentional muscular control,
* or the impulse to constantly “correct” the body.

Relaxation therefore becomes less of an active technique and more of a process of **removing unnecessary interference**.

### 4. The Feeling of Being “Saturated” or “Insufficient”

The body is constantly undergoing internal movement.

In this model, the question is not simply whether there is “more blood” or “less blood.”

Instead, the relevant subjective question is:

> ***Can this tissue receive and circulate internal movement naturally?***

When tissue is:

* excessively compressed,
* excessively expanded,
* locally over-tensioned,
* or held in a fixed configuration,

the person may experience sensations such as:

* stagnation,
* heaviness,
* blockage,
* internal pressure,
* or a lack of movement.

The words “saturated” and “insufficient” are therefore phenomenological descriptions, not medical measurements of blood volume.

They describe a subjective sense that the tissue is either unable to accommodate internal movement or lacks sufficient cohesion for that movement to circulate naturally.

### 5. Why the Perineum, Pelvic Floor, and Deep Abdomen Are Useful Areas to Observe

Certain regions make this phenomenon easier to notice.

Examples include:

* the perineum,
* pelvic floor,
* lower abdomen,
* the area deep behind the navel,
* deeper abdominal structures,
* deep lumbar tissues,
* and the region around the diaphragm.

These areas are difficult to manipulate directly from the surface.

Simply trying to “relax the muscle” may not produce the desired state.

Instead, when unnecessary tension decreases, one may begin to perceive something different:

> ***A deep region that feels gently cohesive while remaining soft and internally mobile.***

The sensation is neither deliberate contraction nor complete collapse.

It is closer to:

> ***the tissue naturally settling into itself.***

### 6. The Role of Pulsation

One of the most important observations in this model is the relationship between bodily relaxation and **pulsation**.

Rather than deliberately moving the body, the deeper tissues may appear to organize themselves subtly in synchrony with the body’s pulse.

The subjective distinction is important.

There is a difference between:

> *“I am contracting this muscle.”*

and:

> *“The tissue seems to gather by itself in response to the pulse.”*

The second sensation is not experienced as an intentional muscular action.

It feels more like the body is revealing a rhythmic movement that was already present.

### 7. Pulse Synchronization Rather Than Forced Movement

When attention is placed on a deep region such as the perineum or the area behind the navel, a subtle rhythmic pattern may become noticeable.

Rather than intentionally contracting:

> *pulse arrives → tissue subtly gathers → pulse passes → tissue releases*

The movement is extremely small.

The important point is not the size of the movement but the **relationship between the movement and the pulse**.

The body seems to organize itself around an internally generated rhythm rather than being driven by deliberate muscular control.

### 8. Why Breathing Is Different

Breathing creates a large and obvious rhythmic movement throughout the body.

This makes breathing a powerful tool for changing bodily sensations.

For example:

* inhalation can create expansion,
* exhalation can create release or contraction.

This can make breathing useful as an entry point for learning bodily relaxation.

However, relying too heavily on breathing can introduce another problem:

> ***The person begins to move the body through breathing rather than allowing the body to organize itself naturally.***

The sequence becomes:

> *breathing → deliberate bodily movement → relaxation sensation*

Whereas the model described here is closer to:

> *reduce unnecessary tension
↓
tissue resistance decreases
↓
internal pulsation becomes easier to perceive
↓
tissue naturally organizes around the pulsation
↓
breathing no longer needs to drive the process*

Thus the goal is not necessarily to use breathing to create relaxation.

It is to create enough freedom for the body’s own rhythms to become apparent.

### 9. Why Conscious Breathing Can Become “Too One-Sided”

Breathing is a relatively large mechanical movement.

When consciously manipulated, attention can become biased toward:

* inhalation,
* exhalation,
* chest expansion,
* abdominal expansion,
* diaphragm movement,
* or deliberately deep breathing.

This can push the body toward a particular mechanical pattern.

In other words:

> *Conscious breathing uses a natural rhythm, but it can also impose a direction onto that rhythm.*

Pulse-based observation is different.

The pulse is smaller, continuous, and generated internally.

Therefore, it may encourage a different relationship:

> ***Rather than making the body move, allow the body to move according to its own rhythm.***

### 10. “The Body Naturally Gathers Around the Pulse”

The key sensation is not simply “feeling the pulse.”

The more interesting phenomenon is:

> ***The deeper tissues seem to organize themselves around the pulse without deliberate effort.***

For example:

* intentionally tightening the pelvic floor creates a muscular contraction,
* intentionally relaxing it can make the tissue collapse,
* but neither may correspond to the desired state.

The third state is:

> *The pulse arrives, the deep tissue subtly gathers, the pulse passes, and the tissue naturally returns.*

This is not experienced as an intentional exercise.

The sense of “I am doing something” becomes weaker.

### 11. Relaxation as “Subtracting Friction”

The simplest way to describe the overall approach may be:

> ***Relaxation is not adding a new movement. It is subtracting friction.***

Instead of adding:

* more breathing,
* more posture control,
* more muscular adjustment,
* more attention,
* more effort,

the practitioner gradually removes:

* unnecessary tension,
* local fixation,
* excessive muscular control,
* forced breathing,
* deliberate postural correction,
* and the impulse to manipulate internal sensations.

Then naturally occurring processes become easier to perceive.

These may include:

* cardiac pulsation,
* respiration,
* subtle muscular activity,
* elastic properties of tissue,
* postural adjustments,
* and autonomic fluctuations.

The fundamental shift is therefore:

> ***Do not force the body to relax. Remove what prevents the body from relaxing naturally.***

### 12. Relaxed Does Not Mean Weak

A key distinction in this model is:

> ***Relaxation and weakness are not the same thing.***

If relaxation simply meant eliminating muscular activity, the body would become unstable.

The state being described here is different.

Unnecessary surface tension decreases while deeper structural cohesion remains.

Therefore, the subjective goal is:

* less unnecessary effort,
* stable posture,
* preserved movement,
* preserved ability to generate force,
* clear mental functioning,
* and easier perception of internal bodily movement.

In other words:

> ***The body can be relaxed without being collapsed.***

### 13. Low Baseline Tension and On-Demand Output

The objective is not to keep the body permanently activated.

Instead:

> ***Reduce unnecessary baseline output while preserving the ability to produce output when needed.***

This can be described as:

> *low resident tension + preserved on-demand output*

rather than:

> *constant relaxation or constant activation.*

The body does not need to maintain a high level of readiness at all times.

The goal is to avoid carrying the energetic cost of “preparing for action” when no action is required.

### 14. Relaxation and Alertness Can Coexist

Another important implication is that bodily relaxation does not necessarily have to produce sleepiness.

The body may become:

* quieter,
* less tense,
* less noisy,

while the brain remains:

* alert,
* responsive,
* clear,
* and capable of high output.

This separates two concepts that are often implicitly linked:

> *physical tension = alertness*

and

> *physical relaxation = sleepiness.*

They do not necessarily have to be the same variable.

The body can potentially operate at low mechanical noise while the nervous system remains sufficiently active for cognition and action.

### 15. Stillness Makes the Sensation Easier to Detect

Large movements can obscure subtle internal movements.

During walking or exercise, the entire body is already moving.

In stillness, however, very small rhythmic changes become easier to notice.

Useful contexts may therefore include:

* sitting quietly,
* standing,
* slow walking,
* or simply remaining still without deliberately correcting posture.

The important principle is:

> ***Do less, and observe what the body does when it is not being constantly adjusted.***

### 16. Posture as an Emergent Result

Posture does not necessarily need to be constructed consciously.

A common approach is:

* straighten the spine,
* lower the shoulders,
* engage the abdomen,
* position the pelvis,
* stabilize the neck.

But every correction introduces another intentional action.

This can create additional tension.

An alternative is to observe:

> *Where does the body feel compressed?
Where is it excessively expanded?
Where does it naturally begin to organize itself?*

From this perspective:

> ***Posture is not something to construct first. It can emerge as a consequence of improved internal organization.***

### 17. The Difference Between Tightening, Collapsing, and Cohesion

Three states can be distinguished.

### Tightening

A deliberate muscular contraction.

### Collapsing

A reduction of tension without sufficient structural organization.

### Cohesion

The deeper tissues feel naturally organized and connected without requiring deliberate contraction.

The third state is the target of this model.

It can be described as:

> ***the body settling into itself without being forced into position.***

This does not necessarily mean that a specific muscle is contracting or that a particular anatomical structure is moving.

It is primarily a phenomenological description of the overall organization of bodily tension.

### 18. The Overall Process

The model can be represented as:

The body is experienced as a three-dimensional biological sponge
↓
Excessive compression or excessive expansion interferes with natural internal movement
↓
Unnecessary tension and fixation are reduced
↓
The tissues return toward an appropriate degree of cohesion
↓
Deep tissues begin to feel naturally organized
↓
Internal pulsation becomes easier to perceive
↓
Tissue movement begins to synchronize subtly with pulsation
↓
Large breathing-driven movements become less necessary
↓
The body can follow its own rhythms
↓
The need to consciously “relax” decreases
↓
The body becomes quieter without losing functional output

### 19. Why This Can Feel Like a Fundamental Change

This approach is difficult to grasp because conventional body training often begins with:

> ***How do I move my muscles correctly?***

The model described here asks a different question:

> ***What condition allows the body to move itself naturally?***

This changes the level of control.

Instead of:

> *consciously controlling muscles
↓
controlling posture
↓
controlling breathing*

the focus shifts toward:

> ***reducing unnecessary interference with the body’s internal organization.***

The subjective result may feel much larger than ordinary flexibility or relaxation.

The sensation of “a different dimension” does not need to imply anything supernatural.

It can simply mean:

> ***The basic strategy for interacting with the body has changed.***

The shift is from:

> *“I move my body.”*

to:

> ***“I create the conditions under which the body can organize itself.”***

### 20. Phenomenological Markers

Possible subjective markers of this state include:

* less need to deliberately relax,
* deeper tissues feeling naturally cohesive,
* internal movement becoming easier to perceive,
* pulsation becoming more apparent,
* less local rigidity,
* stable posture without excessive effort,
* preserved mental clarity,
* preserved ability to generate force,
* reduced need for deliberate breathing control,
* and a gradual disappearance of the feeling that one is “doing a relaxation exercise.”

One particularly important marker is:

> ***The less the person tries to create the state, the more naturally the state appears.***

### 21. A Note on Physiology

The “capillary sponge,” “internal flow,” and “pulse synchronization” concepts are not established physiological mechanisms as described here.

They are conceptual models for organizing subjective experience.

Actual human physiology involves complex interactions among:

* blood flow,
* vascular dynamics,
* tissue fluid,
* lymphatic circulation,
* muscle activity,
* fascia and connective tissue,
* respiration,
* cardiac pulsation,
* venous return,
* autonomic regulation,
* and neural processing.

Therefore:

> *“I feel this sensation” does not automatically mean “this specific physiological mechanism is occurring.”*

The useful question is instead:

> ***Can this phenomenological model reliably describe and predict changes in subjective bodily state?***

That can potentially be investigated through repeated observation and, where appropriate, physiological measurements.

### 22. Questions for Future Observation

The model can be developed further by observing:

1. Which body regions most reliably produce the sensation.
2. How clearly the sensation synchronizes with cardiac pulsation.
3. How it differs from deliberate breathing-based relaxation.
4. How posture affects the sensation.
5. Differences between stillness and movement.
6. The relationship between bodily relaxation and alertness.
7. Whether subjective changes correlate with heart rate or HRV.
8. Whether the state can be maintained for long periods.
9. Whether preserved output can coexist with reduced baseline tension.
10. Whether the sensation becomes increasingly automatic with practice.

### 23. Core Principle

The entire model can be summarized as:

> ***Do not forcibly relax the body. Create the conditions in which the body can naturally organize itself around its own rhythms.***

Or, more specifically:

> ***Think of the body as a living sponge. Do not squeeze it, and do not force it open. Reduce the friction that prevents it from naturally gathering and moving with the pulse.***

The ultimate shift is:

> ***From “relaxing the body”
to “stopping interference with the body’s natural relaxation.”***

That distinction is the central idea of this body-sensation model.

# Short Sleep and “Blood & Spirit” — Separating Subjective QOL, Arousal, and Physiological Recovery
* **File**: 02_short_sleep_and_blood_spirit_separating_subjective.md | **Date**: Sat, 12 Sep 2026 13:18:06 GMT

### 1. Starting Point

Recently, I have unintentionally developed something that could be described as a “short-sleeper-like” pattern.

However, this is not the result of deliberately training myself to sleep less.

I have always been able to put my body into a deeply restful state regardless of how much I had already slept. When I enter a strong body-sensation mode, I can relax and fall asleep quite easily.

At the same time, recently I have almost completely lost the subjective sensation of sleepiness, and maintaining wakefulness is no longer particularly difficult. As a result, my sleep duration has naturally become shorter.

Rather than simply interpreting this as “having less need for sleep,” it is more useful to separate:

* subjective sleepiness
* neural arousal
* bodily rest and recovery
* cognitive activity
* bodily awareness
* subjective quality of life (QOL)[https://medium.com/media/0b958727f0808c42b89d9b4068206ebf/href](https://medium.com/media/0b958727f0808c42b89d9b4068206ebf/href)

### 2. The Personal “Blood / Spirit” Model

“Blood” and “Spirit” are not being proposed here as literal physiological entities or as a direct claim about traditional medical theory.

They are personal phenomenological terms for describing two interacting directions of bodily experience.

“Blood” refers primarily to:

* the physical body
* deep musculature
* capillary-level bodily sensations
* bodily awareness
* rest and recovery
* the tendency toward physical relaxation

“Spirit” refers primarily to:

* the nervous system
* the brain
* cognition
* arousal
* conscious and intellectual activity

In actual physiology, the body and brain cannot be cleanly separated in this way. “Blood / Spirit” is therefore a phenomenological and operational model rather than a scientific classification.

The important observation is that these two directions can, to some extent, be intentionally influenced and their relative balance can be changed.

### 3. The Previous Baseline — “Blood” Dominant

Previously, my baseline tended to be relatively “Blood-dominant.”

I did relatively little to actively stimulate the “Spirit” side of the system. In particular, there were fewer things I wanted to do or actively engage with at the cognitive or neural level.

As a result:

Blood dominant
↓
The body naturally moves toward a chilled/restful state
↓
I lie down
↓
Even without feeling particularly sleepy, I eventually fall asleep

The important point is that this was not necessarily:

“I was sleepy, therefore I slept.”

It was closer to:

“I am not particularly sleepy, but there is little reason to keep stimulating the Spirit side, so if I simply lie down and let the body take over, I naturally fall asleep.”

Therefore, the fact that I previously slept for relatively long periods does not necessarily mean that I had a strong subjective sleep drive.

### 4. The Recent Change — Increasing Stimulation of the “Spirit” Side

Recently, especially through music, I have been intentionally increasing stimulation on the “Spirit” side.

Music is not being treated merely as entertainment.

My ongoing exploration of music concerns whether sound can selectively influence:

* cognitive clarity
* brain activity
* bodily sensations
* spatial perception
* bodily synchronization
* arousal
* neural activity

As a result, the previous balance of:

Blood > Spirit

can shift toward:

Blood ↔ Spirit

or, depending on the situation:

Spirit > Blood

When this happens, the body no longer continuously falls into a strongly chilled or sleep-oriented state. Wakefulness becomes easier to maintain naturally.

### 5. It Is Not “Forcing Myself to Sleep Less”

The current state is not one in which I am resisting sleep.

Rather:

“I can still sleep whenever I want, but there is now enough activity, interest, or stimulation on the Spirit side that I naturally remain awake.”

This is different from conventional “sleep restriction” or “short-sleep training.”

I am not deliberately reducing sleep as the goal.

Rather, my overall state appears to have changed, and shorter sleep duration has emerged as a consequence.

### 6. Subjective Sleepiness and Physiological Sleep Need Are Different

This is one of the most important distinctions.

The absence of subjective sleepiness does not necessarily mean that the body has no physiological need for sleep.

At least five variables should be distinguished:

1. Whether sleepiness is subjectively experienced
2. Whether wakefulness can be maintained
3. Whether cognitive performance can be maintained
4. Whether the body feels physically comfortable
5. Whether physiological recovery from sleep is sufficient

At present, several things are clear from my own experience:

* physiological sleepiness is almost absent
* maintaining wakefulness is not difficult
* familiar cognitive activities remain relatively stable
* bodily homeostasis does not feel significantly disrupted
* subjective QOL is high

However, it is not yet possible to conclude that my actual physiological requirement for sleep has permanently decreased.

### 7. Sleepiness Has Been “Conditioned” or Trained Down

In my own experience, the subjective sensation of sleepiness has been progressively weakened through long-term intentional conditioning.

As a result, the conventional sequence:

“I become sleepy → therefore I sleep”

has become very weak.

The current configuration may therefore be:

The body retains the ability to sleep
+
The subjective signal of sleepiness is almost absent
+
Stimulation of the Spirit side helps maintain wakefulness

This does not mean that the disappearance of sleepiness proves that sleep is unnecessary.

It means that the subjective signal directing me toward sleep has become largely separated from the underlying physiological processes involved in sleep and recovery.

### 8. Familiar Cognition vs. Unfamiliar Cognition

There is also an important difference between familiar and unfamiliar cognitive activity.

For familiar activities, the relevant models of the environment, body, and behavior are already established. Therefore, relatively little cognitive processing is required.

For unfamiliar things, I do not automatically allocate large amounts of cognitive resources unless they become necessary.

This creates something like:

Familiar activity
→ relatively little cognitive resource required
→ wakefulness is easy to maintain

Unfamiliar activity
→ resources are allocated only when necessary
→ Spirit-side activity increases
→ bodily awareness may also become more noticeable

This is relevant because the current reduction in sleepiness may not simply be a matter of “having more energy.” It may also involve how cognitive resources are allocated.

### 9. Body-Sensation Mode and the Parasympathetic-Like Feeling

When I reduce my sleep duration compared with my usual habits, I sometimes notice a somewhat stronger “parasympathetic-like” bodily feeling.

This does not necessarily mean that I am sleepy.

I can remain awake and cognitively functional while simultaneously sensing that the body itself is somewhat inclined toward rest.

This should therefore be distinguished from ordinary sleepiness.

It is also possible that this feeling simply reflects the fact that my body has not yet adapted to the shorter sleep duration.

If shorter sleep and the current Blood/Spirit balance became habitual, this bodily tendency might diminish.

That remains a hypothesis rather than an established conclusion.

### 10. The Importance of the Physical Baseline

Another important feature is that increasing Spirit-side stimulation does not currently seem to produce the level of muscular tension, stiffness, or exhaustion that it might otherwise cause.

Long-term physical work has improved my baseline bodily condition, including:

* deep musculature
* bodily awareness
* posture
* breathing
* nervous-system regulation
* internal muscular tension

As a result:

Spirit stimulation increases
↓
Spirit-side activity rises
↓
The body becomes less strongly rest-dominant
↓
Yet physical homeostasis remains relatively stable

One possible interpretation is that a sufficiently strong physical baseline has developed to support greater neural activity without immediately producing bodily breakdown.

### 11. “Both Blood and Spirit Are Full”

My current subjective assessment is that both Blood and Spirit are relatively well supplied.

On the Spirit side:

* I am alert
* cognition is available
* music can strongly affect my state
* I can engage in things I want to do
* subjective QOL is high

On the Blood side:

* bodily sensations remain accessible
* I can easily move into a deeply restful state
* chronic stiffness or strong fatigue is not dominant
* physical comfort remains relatively good

Therefore, subjective QOL is clearly high.

However:

“Both Blood and Spirit feel full”

does not necessarily mean:

“Every physiological process inside the body is optimal.”

There remains an unknown region that subjective experience cannot directly observe.

### 12. Subjective QOL and Physiological Health Are Separate Axes

This distinction is essential.

A person may have insomnia or frequently complain of being tired while nevertheless remaining active and living a long life.

Conversely, a person may:

* feel energetic
* be highly active
* experience little sleepiness
* have a high subjective QOL

while still having physiological risks that do not appear clearly in subjective experience.

Therefore:

High subjective QOL
≠
Perfect physiological health

Likewise:

Short sleep
≠
Automatically unhealthy

Subjective experience and physiological health need to be treated as separate dimensions.

### 13. “Fatigue” Also Needs to Be Decomposed

When discussing short sleep, simply saying “I am tired” is not sufficiently informative.

Different forms of fatigue should be distinguished:

* sleepiness
* cognitive fatigue
* social fatigue
* muscular fatigue
* autonomic exhaustion
* emotional fatigue
* inadequate physical recovery
* reduced motivation
* fatigue caused simply by prolonged activity

This matters because an apparent relationship between short sleep and fatigue can easily be confounded by other demands placed on the person while awake.

### 14. The Problem with Interpreting Daisuke Hori’s 30-Minute Sleep Experiment

Daisuke Hori has publicly demonstrated a 30-minute-sleep lifestyle for a week through livestreaming.

This can be an interesting case when thinking about extreme short sleep.

However, even if someone appears tired or reports fatigue during such an experiment, it does not automatically follow that:

“30-minute sleep caused the fatigue.”

The experiment also contains:

* long livestream sessions
* continuous responses to chat comments
* conversation
* decision-making
* social interaction
* constant exposure to an audience
* psychological pressure created by the experiment itself

In particular, continuously responding to livestream comments is not equivalent to simply being awake.

It requires sustained cognitive and social processing.

In the Blood/Spirit framework, this may represent a substantial load on the Spirit side.

Therefore, the fatigue caused by reduced sleep must be separated from fatigue caused by prolonged cognitive and social activity.

### 15. What Should Actually Be Compared?

When investigating short sleep, sleep duration alone is insufficient.

The relevant variables include:

* sleep duration
* total waking time
* the quality of waking activity
* cognitive load
* social load
* physical load

For example:

A: 8 hours of sleep + low-load daily life

B: 4 hours of sleep + low-load daily life

C: 4 hours of sleep + high-load work

D: 30 minutes of sleep + low-stimulation environment

E: 30 minutes of sleep + prolonged livestreaming and social interaction

These are not equivalent conditions.

The results from E cannot simply be generalized to D.

### 16. What “Short Sleeper” Means in This Model

The term “short sleeper” is too broad to fully describe the current phenomenon.

At least five different components should be distinguished:

1. Short sleep duration
2. Low subjective sleepiness
3. Ability to maintain wakefulness
4. Ability to maintain cognitive function
5. Reduced physiological need for sleep

At present, my experience strongly supports some elements of 1–4, especially low sleepiness and easy maintenance of wakefulness.

Element 5 remains unknown.

Therefore, the most cautious description is not:

“My physiological need for sleep has definitely decreased.”

It is:

“My subjective sleepiness has become almost absent, and wakefulness can be maintained naturally, so my sleep duration has become shorter.”

### 17. A Lifestyle Based on QOL

The fundamental principle behind this lifestyle is not:

“Maximize health metrics.”

It is:

“Maximize actual quality of life.”

Therefore, a conventional health practice is not automatically adopted simply because it is described as “healthy.”

The relevant question is:

Does this actually improve my:

* quality of life
* ability to act
* bodily comfort
* cognitive state
* sense of well-being
* freedom?

If a modern health practice appears not to contribute to actual QOL, I do not necessarily adopt it merely because it is conventionally recommended.

This is not the same as rejecting modern medicine.

It means that actual effects on my lived condition are given priority over authority or general rules.

### 18. But Risks That Do Not Appear in QOL Are a Separate Issue

There is an important limitation to this approach.

Maximizing subjective QOL and maximizing long-term physiological safety are not necessarily identical goals.

Some physiological variables may have little immediate effect on subjective QOL.

For example, a person may feel comfortable while long-term changes are occurring in areas such as:

* metabolism
* vascular function
* endocrine regulation
* immune function
* tissue repair

These processes may not be directly visible through subjective experience.

Therefore:

“My QOL is high, so I must be completely healthy”

cannot be concluded.

At the same time:

“My lifestyle differs from conventional health advice, so I must be unhealthy”

cannot be concluded either.

The unknown should remain unknown.

### 19. The Most Neutral Current Model

The current hypothesis can be summarized as follows:

Physical baseline is relatively strong
↓
There is sufficient reserve on the Blood side
↓
When Spirit stimulation is low, Blood tends to dominate

Therefore, the hypothesis is not:

“Short sleep makes me healthy.”

It is closer to:

“A change in the Blood/Spirit balance has allowed me to maintain high subjective QOL and wakefulness with less sleep.”

### 20. What Should Be Observed Going Forward?

If this hypothesis is to be examined seriously, sleepiness alone is not enough.

Useful variables include:

* sleep duration

* long-term health markers

The particularly important question is not:

“Am I sleepy?”

but:

“What happens to physical recovery and cognitive function when this short-sleep state is maintained over time?”

### 21. The Core of the Hypothesis

This is not an argument that short sleep is inherently healthy.

The central idea is:

**Sleep, sleepiness, arousal, cognition, physical recovery, and QOL should not be treated as a single variable.**

Human subjective experience provides powerful signals such as:

“I am sleepy.”
“I am tired.”

From this perspective, short sleep is not merely a question of sleep duration.

It becomes a broader question of:

**How does a human being distribute resources between neural activity, bodily regulation, wakefulness, rest, and recovery?**

At present, both Blood and Spirit appear relatively strong in my subjective experience, and subjective QOL is very high.

The most neutral position is therefore:

**Do not assume that short sleep is either inherently healthy or inherently harmful. Instead, treat Spirit, Blood, subjective QOL, arousal, sleepiness, and physiological recovery as partially independent variables and observe how they interact.**

### Cold Exposure, Torso Awareness, and Thermoregulation-A Hypothesis of Whole-Body Integration Rather Than Breath Control

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*Lg_ZJuBhyNdeFR87xnk-ZQ.png)

### 1. Overview of the Observation

During exposure to an extremely strong industrial air-conditioning airflow, a new observation emerged regarding thermoregulation.

The key factor did not appear to be deliberate breath control.

Instead, body temperature seemed easier to maintain when the following conditions were present:

* The internal torso structure from the diaphragm through the abdominal region to the pelvic floor was naturally well-supported or “upright.”
* Awareness of the internal torso remained present as a background condition.
* Attention did not become completely detached from the body.
* Cognitive activity could still occur, but it did not pull awareness entirely into the head.
* Breathing could become extremely small and largely spontaneous.
* Despite the minimal breathing, heart rate gradually increased in response to the cold.
* Whole-body warmth appeared to be maintained.

By contrast, under the same cold airflow, when torso awareness became faint and attention became absorbed in another activity, the body simply became cold.

This suggests a hypothesis:

> ***The key factor in this form of thermoregulation may not be respiratory manipulation itself, but the maintenance of an integrated bodily state in which the torso remains connected to ongoing physiological regulation.***

This remains a personal phenomenological observation and a working hypothesis, not an established physiological conclusion.

### 2. The Most Important Contrast

The observation can be simplified into two contrasting conditions.

### Condition A: Connection with the body is maintained

* The internal torso structure from the diaphragm to the pelvic floor feels naturally organized.
* The torso remains present as a background bodily sensation.
* Attention can engage with the external environment without becoming completely detached from the body.
* Thoughts do not need to stop.
* Breathing does not need to be deliberately manipulated.
* Breathing may become extremely small.
* Heart rate nevertheless appears to increase in response to the cold.
* Whole-body warmth appears to be maintained.

### Condition B: Connection with the body becomes weak

* Attention becomes strongly absorbed in a computer, game, monitor, or another external task.
* Visual processing, thinking, decision-making, and hand movements become dominant.
* Torso and internal bodily sensations fade into the background.
* This does not necessarily involve a large amount of conscious thought.
* Even a relatively “thoughtless” state can involve strong head-centered activity.
* Cold gradually becomes more noticeable.
* The body appears to become physically colder.

The important distinction therefore may not be:

**thinking vs. not thinking**

but rather:

**remaining connected to the body vs. becoming detached from bodily regulation.**

### 3. Thinking and Bodily Disconnection Are Not the Same Thing

A central insight is that the presence or absence of thought does not appear to explain the phenomenon adequately.

For example, a person may be looking at a monitor and have very little explicit verbal thought while still engaging heavily in:

* object recognition
* prediction
* decision-making
* motor control
* external stimulus tracking

Subjectively, this may feel like “not thinking,” while attention is nevertheless strongly concentrated in head-centered processing.

Conversely, thoughts can occur while the body remains continuously present as a background field of sensation.

Therefore, the relevant variable may be better described as:

> ***the distribution of cognitive activity and its degree of coupling with whole-body awareness***

rather than the amount of thought itself.

This distinction is consistent with the broader observation that reducing thought is not necessarily the same thing as shifting away from excessive head-centered processing.

### 4. What “Torso Awareness” Means in This Model

The experience is closer to an integrated internal organization involving:

* diaphragm

* abdominal cavity
* lumbar region
* pelvic floor
* psoas / deep hip region
* inner thighs and hip creases
* cervical base
* occipital region
* eyes and orbital structures

The important feature is not that each structure is consciously monitored.

Rather, these regions seem to become integrated into a continuous bodily system.

The phrase “the abdomen is upright” or “the torso is standing” therefore refers to:

> ***a naturally supported deep-body organization extending from the diaphragm toward the pelvic floor, without excessive muscular effort.***

This is different from deliberately bracing the abdominal muscles.

The intended state is not rigidity but efficient internal support with reduced unnecessary effort.

### 5. Breathing May Be an Output Rather Than the Primary Cause

This is only a hypothesis. No direct measurements of metabolic rate, core temperature, ventilation, or oxygen consumption were made during the observation.

Or, more simply:

> ***Do not try to “make the body warm.” Create a state in which the body can regulate itself without unnecessary interference.***

This makes the cold-exposure observation potentially important beyond cold tolerance itself. It may provide a practical environmental stress test for a broader hypothesis concerning whole-body integration, low baseline noise, and preserved on-demand physiological responsiveness.

### A Hypothesis of Music-Based Brain–Body Regulation-An Integrated Model of Acoustic Structure, Prediction, Bodily Synchronization, Spatial Perception, and Immersion

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*FnW4hzpSuD1PZJqrj1IXsA.png)

### 0. Status and Purpose of This Document

This document is a working hypothesis about how specific musical structures may influence cognition, bodily states, emotion, attention, arousal, and immersive experience.

Some components are compatible with established findings in music cognition, auditory perception, neuroscience, and motor synchronization. Other components are hypotheses derived primarily from personal phenomenological observations.

These should not be treated as equivalent.

The purpose of this document is not to establish a finished theory, but to decompose the idea of “music regulating the brain and body” into potentially testable musical parameters.

### 1. The Initial Observation

When humans listen to music, they do not merely understand the meaning of the song.

Musical structures themselves can produce experiences such as:

* bodily movement or an urge to move
* changes in breathing or bodily rhythm
* tension and relaxation
* a sense of spatial expansion
* immersion
* increased mental clarity
* emotional activation
* anticipation
* a feeling that the body is “coming online”

These responses do not necessarily require lyrical or narrative meaning.

This suggests a possible pathway:

**Acoustic structure → nervous system / body → subjective experience**

Music may therefore function not only as a medium for communicating meaning, but also as a structured stimulus capable of directly modulating perceptual, motor, emotional, and bodily processes.

### 2. Music Should Not Be Treated as a Single “Emotion” Variable

It is common to describe music using global labels such as:

* sad
* happy
* exciting
* emotional
* relaxing

However, this model treats musical response as a collection of partially distinct pathways.

Possible pathways include:

1. Harmonic and predictive processing
2. Rhythmic and bodily synchronization
3. Timbre and auditory-sensory processing
4. Spatial and bodily-boundary perception
5. Memory and semantic association
6. Emotional evaluation
7. Motor activation and arousal

These pathways are not independent. They interact continuously.

Therefore, two pieces that produce a similar subjective emotion may do so through substantially different underlying mechanisms.

### 3. Central Hypothesis: Music Can Selectively Drive Different Pathways

Music can be understood as:

**a stimulus that temporally organizes multiple neural and bodily pathways.**

For example:

**Sound → prediction → maintained expectation → subtle violation → tension → resolution**

may preferentially engage cognitive and emotional processing.

By contrast:

**Sound → periodicity → temporal prediction → motor synchronization → bodily activation**

may preferentially engage bodily and motor processes.

Another pathway might be:

**Sound → spatial information → auditory expansion → bodily boundary perception → immersion**

Thus, instead of simply asking:

> *“How strongly does this music affect me?”*

we can ask:

> *“Which pathway is being affected, and how?”*

### 4. Harmony and Melody: Effects on Cognitive Processing

In preliminary generative-music testing, the harmonic version produced the strongest subjective impression of “affecting the brain.”

One possible explanation is the predictive structure created by harmony.

When listening to music, the listener may implicitly predict what sound or chord will occur next.

A musical structure can therefore create:

**prediction → slight deviation → tension → continued expectation → resolution**

Potentially effective musical elements include:

* semitone or whole-tone movement
* narrow melodic intervals
* minor-second tension
* suspended notes
* incomplete cadences
* delayed resolution
* rising melodic phrases
* descending melodic phrases
* recurring motifs with small variations
* chromatic or stepwise movement in inner voices

The important factor may not simply be “complex harmony,” but the controlled manipulation of expectation.

### 5. Voice Leading May Matter More Than Chord Labels

A chord progression can be described using chord names such as:

C → Am → F → G

However, subjective experience may depend strongly on how individual voices move between those chords.

For example, if one voice remains almost stationary while another moves by a semitone, the listener may experience a subtle sense of motion that cannot be fully described by the chord labels themselves.

This suggests a distinction between:

**Chord progression**

and

**the temporal movement of individual pitches.**

The latter may contribute to an auditory analogue of movement or bodily sensation.

### 6. Tension and Release

One fundamental structure is:

**tension → suspension → release**

The important factor may be the change between states rather than the absolute amount of tension.

A continuously dissonant piece may eventually become perceptually normalized.

By contrast:

**stability → instability → expectation → resolution**

creates a dynamic contrast.

Therefore, musical stimulation may be better understood as the design of **state transitions**, rather than the maximization of stimulation.

### 7. Rhythm: The Bodily Synchronization Pathway

Rhythm may engage a different pathway from harmony.

When a listener hears a periodic pattern, the motor and perceptual systems can predict the timing of future events.

A possible sequence is:

**periodicity → temporal prediction → motor prediction → bodily synchronization**

This may explain why rhythmic music can produce an experience of the body “coming online.”

In the preliminary experiment, a rhythm-focused version produced a noticeable feeling that the body was becoming activated.

Interestingly, this was accompanied by an impression of increased mental clarity rather than increased cognitive clutter.

### 8. Bodily Activation and Cognitive Stimulation May Be Separate Axes

A common assumption is:

**stronger stimulation = stronger cognitive activation**

But the preliminary observation suggests that these variables may be partially separable.

It may be possible to have:

**bodily activation ↑**

while simultaneously having:

**cognitive clutter ↓**

Possible explanations include:

* stabilization of motor timing
* reduction of unnecessary motor preparation
* more stable attentional anchoring
* improved coupling between perception and movement
* reduced internal fluctuation

Thus, music might sometimes increase bodily activation without increasing subjective cognitive load.

This is an important hypothesis for future testing.

### 9. Timbre and Spatial Processing: The Immersion Pathway

Spatial audio processing may produce effects distinct from those produced by harmony or rhythm.

Relevant parameters include:

* reverberation
* stereo width
* perceived distance
* depth
* lateral movement
* diffusion
* frequency-dependent spatial distribution

Dry sounds tend to feel localized:

> *“The sound is there.”*

Long reverberation and spatial diffusion can instead create:

> *“The sound exists around me.”*

This may contribute to:

**acoustic space → spatial perception → bodily boundary perception → immersion**

The relationship between acoustic space and bodily self-perception remains a hypothesis.

### 10. Spatial Processing and Bodily Synchronization

The preliminary observations suggested different subjective effects:

**Spatial version:**
greater spatial expansion and immersive qualities.

**Rhythmic synchronization version:**
stronger bodily activation and a clearer mental state.

A tentative distinction is:

**Spatial processing → external space / bodily boundary**

**Rhythmic synchronization → internal movement / arousal**

These should not be treated as established neurological divisions. They are useful working categories for experimentation.

### 11. Timbre

Timbre should not be treated simply as “which instrument is playing.”

Relevant acoustic properties include:

* attack
* decay
* sustain
* release
* harmonic spectrum
* noise components
* frequency distribution
* temporal density

Soft pads and strings can blur the boundaries of individual sounds and create continuous, spatial experiences.

Piano and percussion have clearer attacks and therefore stronger temporal definition.

A combination of:

**soft background textures + clear transient events**

may provide both:

* spatial continuity
* bodily / temporal clarity

### 12. Bass

Low-frequency content may play an important role in bodily experience.

Potentially relevant features include:

* deep bass
* smooth bass lines
* descending bass movement
* ascending bass movement
* pedal tones

Descending bass lines may subjectively suggest:

* grounding
* sinking
* settling

Ascending bass lines may suggest:

* rising
* lifting
* activation

A pedal tone creates another interesting structure:

**stable low-frequency foundation + changing upper harmony**

This may allow the listener to experience a stable bodily base while cognitive or harmonic space changes above it.

### 13. Silence and Absence of Sound

Stimulation does not require continuous sound.

Silence can create:

**expectation → anticipation → absence → renewed input**

A sudden reduction in sound after a dense passage can make the silence itself perceptually salient.

For example:

**dense harmony + high energy → sudden silence → minimal sound**

can create a strong state transition.

Thus, musical design should include both the presence and absence of sound.

### 14. Musical Development as State Transition

Musical development should not be understood simply as:

**quiet → loud → climax**

Instead, it can be modeled as:

**state A → expectation → state B → tension → state C → integration → release**

A potentially useful structure is:

1. Quiet beginning
2. Small recurring motif
3. Subtle variation
4. Accumulation of expectation
5. Introduction of bodily rhythm
6. Harmonic expansion
7. Expansion of spatial field
8. Controlled climax
9. Sudden reduction
10. Spacious afterglow

### 15. The Goal Is Integration Rather Than Maximum Stimulation

A central refinement of the model is that the objective should not be:

> *“Stimulate the brain and body as strongly as possible.”*

Instead, the goal may be:

* moderate cognitive engagement
* appropriate bodily activation
* coherent spatial perception
* reduced interference between pathways

This produces a hypothetical sequence:

**stimulation → synchronization → reduced friction → clarity**

The optimal musical state may therefore not correspond to maximum intensity.

### 16. A Preliminary Brain–Body Regulation Model

A simplified model is:

### Harmony

Prediction → tension → resolution
↓
Cognitive and emotional activation

### Rhythm

Periodicity → temporal prediction → synchronization
↓
Bodily activation and arousal

### Bass

Low-frequency foundation and movement
↓
Bodily grounding

### Timbre

Harmonic spectrum and temporal texture
↓
Sensory quality

### Spatial processing

Distance, width, reverberation
↓
Immersion and bodily-boundary perception

### Musical development

State transitions
↓
Integration and release

These processes interact rather than operate independently.

### 17. The Concept of “Pathways”

A particularly important personal observation is the distinction between:

**a response disappearing**

and

**a pathway becoming less frequently activated.**

A person may experience a substantial reduction in the spontaneous bodily or emotional response normally associated with music, while the underlying pathway remains available.

A sufficiently specific stimulus may then reactivate it.

This suggests a model in which different pathways have different activation thresholds:

* Pathway A: easily activated
* Pathway B: weakly activated
* Pathway C: rarely reaches conscious awareness
* Pathway D: strongly activated only by specific stimuli

Music may therefore function as a tool for selectively probing these pathways.

### 18. Why AI-Generated Music Is Interesting

AI-generated music provides an interesting experimental environment.

Human composers often create music through personal intention, expression, memory, and subjective experience.

Generative models can instead reproduce statistical regularities learned from large amounts of music.

These regularities include:

* harmonic progressions
* melodic patterns
* rhythmic structures
* timbral combinations
* transitions
* climaxes
* resolutions

Therefore, AI-generated music may sometimes produce highly concentrated combinations of musical structures that humans tend to respond to.

This does not mean that an AI “understands human emotion.”

A more conservative interpretation is:

> *AI systems can statistically reconstruct musical structures that frequently occur in music that humans find perceptually and emotionally compelling.*

### 19. AI Music as a Pathway-Exploration Tool

Generative music can therefore be used as an exploratory tool.

The question becomes:

> *“Which musical parameters activate which subjective pathway?”*

For example:

### Condition A

Change harmony while keeping rhythm, lyrics, tempo, and arrangement similar.

### Condition B

Change rhythm while keeping harmony similar.

### Condition C

Change timbre.

### Condition D

Change spatial processing.

This allows the listener to compare subjective responses while controlling some variables.

Possible measurements include:

* bodily activation
* mental clarity
* emotional intensity
* immersion
* spatial expansion
* urge to move
* tension
* relaxation
* attentional stability
* subjective cognitive load

### 20. Preliminary Generative-Music Observations

### Harmonic version

Produced the strongest subjective impression of “affecting the brain.”

Possible contributors:

* prediction
* tension
* delayed resolution
* voice leading
* melodic expectation
* emotional evaluation

### Spatial version

Produced the next strongest effect.

Possible contributors:

* spatial immersion
* acoustic expansion
* perceived distance
* bodily-boundary effects

### Bodily synchronization version

Produced a strong feeling that the body was becoming active or “coming online.”

At the same time, the mind felt relatively clear.

Possible contributors:

* rhythmic synchronization
* motor prediction
* bodily arousal
* attentional stabilization

These observations are phenomenological reports, not established neurophysiological conclusions.

### 21. Genre as an Outer Layer

Musical genre can be treated as an “outer layer” rather than as the fundamental mechanism.

The same underlying regulation principles can potentially be implemented in different genres.

Examples:

**Ambient**

* spatial immersion
* clarity
* gradual expansion

**Jazz**

* harmonic prediction
* subtle variation
* complex voice leading

**House**

* bodily synchronization
* repetition
* pulse

**Post-rock**

* long-term accumulation
* expansion
* release

**Shoegaze**

* spatial immersion
* dense timbral fields

**Speedcore**

* extremely fast bodily synchronization
* rapid harmonic tension and release
* high-energy spatial openness

The genre changes the surface expression while the underlying mechanisms may remain similar.

### 22. Speedcore as an Extreme Test Case

Simply increasing BPM may produce:

**stimulation → fatigue / pressure**

rather than clarity.

A more interesting configuration is:

**very fast rhythm**
+
**bright harmony**
+
**clear high-frequency transients**
+
**deep but controlled bass**
+
**rapid tension and resolution**
+
**wide spatial field**

The goal is not aggressive intensity, but:

> *high-speed movement that feels light, exhilarating, lucid, and physically energizing.*

This can be conceptualized as:

**high-speed bodily synchronization + harmonic release + spatial openness**

### 23. Why Minimize Lyrical Meaning?

Lyrics introduce strong semantic, autobiographical, and emotional associations.

If the goal is to study the effects of acoustic structure itself, semantic content should be minimized.

Possible strategies include:

* meaningless syllables
* invented words
* vowel-rich vocalization
* short repeated phrases
* vocals treated as an instrument

Using Latin, Sanskrit, or other real languages introduces additional semantic and cultural associations.

For controlled experimentation, invented phonetic material is therefore preferable.

### 24. Connection to Cognitive Load and Secondary Reaction

This model can be connected to a broader distinction between:

**primary processing**

and

**secondary reaction to that processing.**

In music:

**sound → perception**

may be followed by:

**perception → evaluation → self-reference → additional cognitive activity**

The proposed ideal is not necessarily to suppress perception.

Instead, it may be possible to create music that:

* establishes useful perceptual and bodily activity
* supports synchronization
* maintains attention
* minimizes unnecessary secondary cognitive reaction

This would correspond to:

**active perception without excessive secondary processing.**

### 25. Connection to Flow

The bodily synchronization observations may have some conceptual overlap with flow.

Flow is often associated with:

* focused attention
* coupling between action and perception
* altered time perception
* reduced self-evaluation
* highly efficient action

In music:

**rhythm → prediction → synchronization → preparation for the next event → continuous perception/action coupling**

could potentially create a related low-friction processing state.

However, rhythmic synchronization and flow should not be treated as identical concepts.

### 26. Overall Integrated Hypothesis

The most comprehensive current hypothesis is:

> *Music is not merely a carrier of meaning. It is a temporally organized stimulus capable of selectively engaging predictive processing, emotion, bodily synchronization, spatial perception, and arousal.*

A further hypothesis is:

> *An optimal musical experience may not be one in which stimulation is maximized, but one in which multiple pathways are activated with appropriate intensity and timing while minimizing interference between them.*

Under this model:

**Harmony**
→ cognitive prediction and emotional processing

**Rhythm**
→ bodily synchronization and arousal

**Bass**
→ bodily grounding

**Timbre**
→ sensory texture

**Spatial processing**
→ immersion and bodily-boundary perception

**Musical development**
→ state transitions

The ultimate target is not maximum stimulation, but a possible state characterized by:

**Mind**

* lucid
* engaged
* spacious

**Body**

* awake
* grounded
* coordinated

**Attention**

* naturally stable

**Spatial experience**

* open

**Emotion**

* present without becoming excessively fixed

### 27. Future Experimental Questions

1. How does changing only harmony affect subjective experience?
2. How does changing only rhythm affect subjective experience?
3. How does timbre alter bodily versus cognitive effects?
4. How does spatial processing alter immersion?
5. Can the same underlying structure produce different effects in different genres?
6. How does BPM affect bodily activation and cognitive clarity?
7. How does harmonic complexity relate to the subjective feeling of “brain stimulation”?
8. Is stronger bodily synchronization associated with greater subjective mental clarity?
9. What happens when semantic meaning is completely removed from lyrics?
10. Does the effect diminish with prolonged listening?
11. Does repetition increase prediction and therefore change the subjective experience over time?
12. Do AI-generated songs and human-composed songs produce different effects when their musical structures are matched?
13. Can individual “pathways” be identified through repeated controlled listening experiments?
14. Can musical parameters be systematically optimized for a desired brain–body state?

### 28. Important Limitations

This is currently a hypothesis model grounded partly in personal phenomenological observation.

It should not be interpreted as establishing simple mappings such as:

> *“This chord activates this brain region.”*

Nor does it establish that music can reliably “optimize the brain and body.”

A more appropriate scientific question is:

> *How do changes in specific musical parameters alter subjective cognitive, bodily, emotional, and attentional states?*

The distinction between phenomenological observation and established neurophysiological mechanism should be maintained throughout future investigation.

### 29. One-Sentence Summary

Music can be understood not only as a carrier of meaning, but as a structured temporal stimulus that coordinates prediction, tension, release, bodily synchronization, spatial perception, and arousal across multiple cognitive–bodily pathways.

The most interesting possibility is that music may be used not simply to increase stimulation, but to activate the brain and body in complementary ways while reducing friction between them, potentially producing a state that is simultaneously **clear, awake, grounded, spacious, and integrated**.

# Appearance, Frameworks, Cross-Domain Translation, and Truth — An Inquiry into What Appears Now and…

### Appearance, Frameworks, Cross-Domain Translation, and Truth — An Inquiry into What Appears Now and What We Call “Truth”

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*s_lyH5XOrWu4P5L7RhnC_g.png)

### 1. Starting Point — What Appears Now Is Basically Not Chosen

What appears as the “now” at any given moment is, for the most part, not something an individual freely chooses.

Sounds are heard, visual phenomena appear, bodily sensations arise, thoughts emerge, people are encountered, events occur, memories surface, and so on. Some appearance occurs first, and only afterward does a person conceptualize it, identify it, or assign meaning to it.

From this perspective, the question “What should appear now?” is already somewhat removed from the structure of appearance itself.

In an extreme sense, one could say:

> *“Whatever appears now is fine.”*

This does not mean that “anything one does produces the same result.” Rather, because what appears cannot fundamentally be chosen at will, actual relationships arise between whatever has appeared and the individual, and further events unfold through those relationships.
[https://medium.com/media/6269f639a4cf46c447d45b11b001b9e9/href](https://medium.com/media/6269f639a4cf46c447d45b11b001b9e9/href)

### 2. The Individual Has a Starting Point Called “This”

Within this flow, human beings select something and treat it as “this,” thereby establishing a starting point from which to apprehend the world.

For example:

* “I begin with this body.”
* “I begin with this consciousness.”
* “I begin with this problem.”
* “I begin with the pursuit of this desire.”

Yet this starting point is not necessarily something freely chosen.

The particular “this” that becomes the individual’s starting point emerges within a network of conditions and relationships involving the body, memory, environment, education, preceding events, chance, habits, and so on.

In this sense, an individual may be understood not as a completely independent initial point separate from the world, but as something that temporarily functions as a starting point within a network of relationships.

### 3. Relationships Arise from the Starting Point, and Further Appearances Arise from Those Relationships

The structure can be described roughly as follows:

Something appears
↓
The individual selects something within it as “this”
↓
Relationships arise around that starting point
↓
Events emerge from those relationships
↓
Those events appear as the next “now”
↓
Something within that is again selected as “this”

Rather than viewing the world as a fixed collection of objects that already exist in predetermined relationships, it may therefore be viewed as a continuous chain of appearances and relationships.

When it rains, relationships with the rain arise.

When one encounters another person, relationships with that person arise.

When hunger appears, a relationship with food arises.

When a thought appears, a relationship with that thought arises.

Further appearances then emerge from these relationships.

### 4. “Because A, Therefore B” Is Shaped After the Fact

From this perspective, causal statements such as “A caused B” cannot simply be regarded as lines of causality already inscribed in reality itself.

For example:

> *“The road became wet because it rained.”*

This statement has already established units such as:

* rain
* road
* becoming wet
* cause
* effect

In reality, countless conditions and relationships may be involved simultaneously:

temperature, humidity, amount of rainfall, material of the road, drainage, wind, time, previous rainfall, and so on.

Human beings select manageable units from this complexity, such as “rain” and “the road becoming wet,” and establish a causal relationship between them.

Therefore, a proposition such as:

> *“A because B”*

is not necessarily false.

However, it becomes meaningful within a framework that has already established A and B as distinguishable units and “cause” and “effect” as a meaningful relationship.

### 5. What Can Be Spoken About Has Already Been Shaped into a Speakable Granularity

Whenever we say something about the world, we are not describing the entirety of the world without mediation.

We have already determined, implicitly or explicitly:

* what counts as one object,
* what distinctions matter,
* what can be ignored,
* what temporal scale is relevant,
* and which relationships are significant.

Only after this shaping does it become possible to say that “this is related to that” or that “this causes that.”

Thus:

> *What can be spoken about is a world already shaped into a granularity that can be spoken about.*

This does not mean that every explanation is false.

Rather, each explanation operates within a particular granularity, purpose, and context.

Science selects a granularity suitable for measurement and comparison.

Everyday life selects a granularity suitable for action.

Philosophy selects a granularity suitable for conceptual manipulation.

Literature may select a granularity at which meaning and emotion become salient.

### 6. Problems Arise When Philosophy, Philology, Science, and Individual Practice Are Compared on a Single Scale

From this perspective, statements such as the following can have local validity but become problematic when turned into universal hierarchies:

* “Philosophy is overly intellectual and lacks experience.”
* “Philology is detached from actual practice.”
* “Science possesses enormous amounts of data.”
* “Individual practice is merely one person’s experience.”

For example, the statement:

> *“Someone is overly intellectual, therefore they lack experience”*

is conceptually strange.

That person necessarily has some phenomena appearing to them.

Thoughts, bodily sensations, emotions, reading, conversations, and so on are all part of what appears within that person’s present experience.

Thus, “lacking experience” may more precisely mean:

> *“They have not sufficiently observed or practiced a particular kind of experience.”*

Moreover, even the statement “they have experience” already treats “experience” as a particular conceptual unit.

### 7. “Science Has Vast Data, While an Individual Has Only One Person’s Worth” Also Depends on the Framework

Science takes information from many people, conditions, measurements, replications, and observations and shapes it into forms that can be compared and, ideally, independently examined.

In this sense, science has “vast amounts of data.”

An individual, by contrast, can be said to possess only one person’s worth of data.

But from another granularity, that individual is continuously situated within an enormous stream of changes that appear only within that individual’s own system.

For example:

* bodily sensations,
* visual phenomena,
* sounds,
* thoughts,
* memories,
* subtle muscular tension,
* shifts of attention,
* interactions with the environment.

Of course, these cannot simply be equated with scientific data in the technical sense.

The important point is:

> *Scientific data and individual experience are not simply competing quantities of data.*

Science adopts a framework in which many observations are transformed into comparable forms.

An individual experiences an enormous stream of changes from within a single system.

Thus the simple opposition

> *science = large amount
individual = small amount*

does not adequately describe the difference.

### 8. Science, Individual Experience, Philosophy, and Philology Are Different Ways of Cutting Out the Same Appearance

From here, oppositions such as:

* science vs. individual
* theory vs. experience
* text vs. practice
* objective vs. subjective
* intellect vs. body

can themselves be relativized.

Each involves different decisions about what to extract, what to make comparable, and what to leave aside.

Therefore, when one framework criticizes another by saying:

> *“You do not have X,”*

the statement may often mean:

> *“Within the framework I am using, X has not been extracted or foregrounded.”*

Rather than ranking different domains, it may be more useful to ask:

> *What does each way of cutting up the world make visible, and what does it make invisible?*

### 9. What Are Humans Doing When They Bring Different Domains into Contact?

Different domains do not necessarily exist as independent entities that humans subsequently connect.

Science, individual practice, texts, philosophy, religion, and so forth can all be understood as frameworks that cut out aspects of appearance in different ways.

When people bring different domains together, what happens can be described as:

> *Taking something extracted within one framework and establishing a correspondence with something extracted within another framework.*

For example:

Individual bodily experience
→ “This particular change occurred.”

Science
→ “If this is expressed in measurable variables, it looks like this.”

Historical texts
→ “Something resembling this has been described in this way.”

Philosophy
→ “This conceptual distinction may describe this difference in experience.”

### 10. Cross-Domain Work Involves Translation

One of the central things humans do when connecting different frameworks is translation.

For example:

> *“This bodily sensation may correspond to this scientific concept.”*

> *“This description in an ancient text may be understood, in contemporary terms, as referring to this kind of phenomenon.”*

> *“This philosophical distinction may help describe this difference in actual experience.”*

However, translation does not mean identity.

The “this” in framework A does not become completely identical to the “this” in framework B.

Rather, one establishes relationships such as:

* correspondence,
* similarity,
* partial explanatory coverage,
* analogy,
* functional equivalence within a particular context.

Therefore, what is interesting about cross-domain work is not necessarily the claim:

> *“Everything has finally been unified into one truth.”*

It may instead be:

> *“Why does this appear in this particular way within framework A and in a different way within framework B?”*

### 11. Over-Unifying Frameworks Creates Problems

If science, individual practice, philology, philosophy, religion, and other domains are evaluated according to a single universal scale, the question inevitably becomes:

> *“Which one is really seeing reality?”*

But each domain is cutting out appearance in a different way.

Therefore, it is not necessary to simply conclude:

> *“Science is right, individual practice is right, and philology is right.”*

A more precise statement would be:

> *“Each is cutting out what appears in a different way.”*

If there is to be a unification, it should not necessarily be a unification of their conclusions.

Rather, it may be a unification at a higher level:

> *Why do different frameworks make different aspects of appearance visible?*

### 12. Appearance Cannot Be Placed on the Same Level as the Domains That Describe It

There is an important asymmetry here.

It is possible to speak of:

* science,
* philosophy,
* philology,
* individual experience,

as frameworks through which appearance is handled.

But placing “appearance” beside these as one more domain is problematic.

Science, philosophy, philology, and individual experience are themselves distinctions established within appearance.

One can therefore provisionally describe the structure as:

Appearance
↓
Scientific, philosophical, philological, individual, etc. ways of cutting it up
↓
Comparison, translation, and connection between those frameworks

But the moment “appearance” itself is treated as one more domain, it becomes another conceptual framework about appearance.

Thus, even saying:

> *“Appearance is prior to all these frameworks”*

is itself already a particular philosophical description.

### 13. If Frameworks Were Removed, Could Everything Be Called “Truth-Like”?

This leads to the intuition:

> *If frameworks could be removed, perhaps everything could be regarded as “truth-like.”*

Here, however, the meaning of “truth” matters.

If truth means “a correct proposition,” then the statement becomes problematic.

But if truth refers to something prior to the distinction between true and false, the idea becomes more coherent.

Consider:

* rain falling,
* hunger arising,
* a thought appearing,
* encountering another person,
* bodily tension,
* writing an equation,
* reading an ancient text,
* conducting a scientific experiment,
* the appearance of a sense of self.

There is no need to initially divide these into:

> *“This is truth”
“This is not truth.”*

The true/false distinction itself requires a framework.

Therefore, if one attempts to move toward the limit where frameworks are minimized, what remains is not:

> *“Only true things exist.”*

Rather:

> *“Something appears before the distinction between truth and falsehood is established.”*

In this sense, saying that “everything is truth-like” does not mean that everything is equally correct.

It means that nothing needs to be excluded in advance as “not truth” before a particular framework has been established.

### 14. “Everything Is Truth-Like” Is Not the Same as “Everything Is True”

Statements such as:

> *“Snow is white”*

can be evaluated as true or false once the relevant conditions and definitions have been established.

But asking of an appearance itself:

> *“Is this truth?”*

is a different kind of question.

The true/false distinction is already part of the framework that turns something into a proposition.

Thus, “truth-like” in this discussion refers to something prior to the assertion:

> *“This is a correct description of reality.”*

The appearance itself does not necessarily arrive already labeled as true or false.

### 15. Yet Saying “Remove All Frameworks” Is Itself a Framework

There is an unavoidable reflexivity here.

The statement:

> *“If all frameworks are removed, everything becomes truth-like”*

is itself a framework once it is expressed as a proposition.

Likewise:

* “Appearance has no hierarchy.”
* “Everything exists within relationships.”
* “Truth exists prior to frameworks.”

These are themselves conceptual constructions once articulated.

Therefore, it is probably impossible to verbally occupy a completely framework-free position.

What is possible, however, is to recognize:

> *“The framework I am currently using is not identical to appearance itself.”*

This is crucial.

The goal is not necessarily to escape frameworks completely.

It is to recognize that:

> *“This explanation is one way of cutting up appearance, rather than appearance itself.”*

This makes it more difficult to absolutize any particular framework.

### 16. The Meaning of “Truth-Seeking” Changes

From this perspective, statements such as:

* “Science discovers truth.”
* “Philosophy searches for truth.”
* “Buddhism discovered truth.”
* “An individual experiences truth through practice.”

can all make sense within their respective frameworks.

But from a more encompassing perspective, each may instead be understood as:

> *A different way of selecting, relating, and describing what appears.*

Truth therefore need not be regarded as something possessed exclusively by one privileged domain.

Different forms of inquiry can disclose different forms of intelligibility within different frameworks.

As frameworks themselves become relativized, the claim:

> *“Only this framework captures truth”*

becomes increasingly difficult to maintain.

### 17. But This Does Not Mean “Everything Is the Same”

An important qualification is necessary.

The fact that nothing must be excluded before a framework is established does not mean:

> *“All descriptions are equally correct.”*

Scientific measurements, dreams, mathematical proofs, and individual sensations do not need to be treated as identical.

Once they are placed within particular frameworks, distinctions naturally arise concerning:

* truth and falsity,
* validity,
* reproducibility,
* explanatory power,
* practical effectiveness,
* predictive success,
* usefulness.

Therefore, two levels should not be confused:

> *Before a framework: there is no necessary basis for imposing a hierarchy of truth/non-truth.*

> *Within a framework: distinctions and evaluations naturally arise.*

### 18. Overall Structure

The entire line of thought can be represented as follows.

#### Layer 1: Appearance

What appears cannot fundamentally be chosen.

Something appears as the present.

At this level, distinctions such as true/false, meaningful/meaningless, subject/object, theory/practice, and so forth have not yet necessarily been established.

↓

#### Layer 2: Extraction

The individual selects something as “this.”

Objects, self, body, problem, event, and so forth are extracted at a particular granularity.

↓

#### Layer 3: Relationships

Relationships arise among the extracted units.

Cause/effect, subject/object, inside/outside, goal/means, and so on.

↓

#### Layer 4: Frameworks

These distinctions are further organized according to particular purposes.

Science, philosophy, philology, individual practice, religion, everyday knowledge, etc.

↓

#### Layer 5: Cross-Domain Translation

Correspondences and translations are established between different frameworks.

This does not imply complete identity.

↓

#### Layer 6: Relativization of Frameworks

The claim that one framework alone captures reality itself becomes questionable.

Each framework is recognized as a particular way of cutting up appearance.

↓

#### Layer 7: Reconsideration of Truth

“Truth” need not be understood only as a correct proposition.

Prior to the true/false distinction, there is simply appearance.

In this sense, nothing appearing needs to be excluded in advance as “not truth.”

“Everything is truth-like” does not mean “everything is equally correct.”

It means that the appearance itself does not necessarily arrive already divided according to a hierarchy of truth and non-truth.

### 19. Core Insight

The core of this inquiry is not to propose one new answer to the question:

> *“What is the truth?”*

Rather, it is to examine how distinctions such as:

* truth,
* experience,
* science,
* individual,
* philosophy,
* text,
* objectivity,
* subjectivity

are themselves established through particular frameworks.

Statements such as:

> *“Science has data, whereas the individual does not.”*

> *“Philosophy lacks experience.”*

> *“Practice is what is truly real.”*

> *“Texts are the most accurate.”*

may all have meaning within particular frameworks.

But when these claims are transformed into universal hierarchies about reality itself, the framework is easily mistaken for reality.

Thus, the important question becomes:

> *“At what granularity am I currently cutting something out, and what framework am I using to do so?”*

From there, one can move further backward:

> *What appears cannot fundamentally be chosen.*

> *The individual selects something as “this.”*

> *Relationships arise from that starting point.*

> *Further appearances emerge from those relationships.*

Human beings then construct various frameworks — science, philosophy, philology, religion, practice, and others — to work with those appearances.

Therefore, crossing domains does not necessarily mean integrating everything into a single unified truth.

It can instead mean observing:

> *“How does something that appears similar take different forms within different frameworks?”*

And when “truth” itself is pushed as far as possible away from any particular framework, even the proposition “what truth is” becomes recognizable as another framework.

What remains is not necessarily a final metaphysical answer, but the recognition that the distinction between truth and non-truth is itself something that arises within a way of cutting up what appears.

In this sense, the intuition that “everything is truth-like” should not be understood as the claim that every proposition is equally valid.

It is better understood as the intuition that:

> ***Before we impose a particular framework of truth, there is no obvious reason to exclude any appearance from reality as inherently ‘non-truth.’***

The inquiry therefore moves away from finding a single privileged description of reality and toward understanding the relationship between **appearance, the frameworks through which it is articulated, and the limits created by those frameworks themselves**.

### The Hidden Structure of “Boring Initial Investment” and the Point Where Everyday Life Itself Becomes Practice

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*YZwjr7igV_H1KDdmPJD0gQ.png)

### 1. Starting Point of the Inquiry

This inquiry began with a piece of writing about a common teaching pattern found across three seemingly different fields: hands-on treatment, martial arts, and meditation.

The original writer points out that the teaching approaches they are currently exposed to share a common characteristic:

They initially devote most of their resources to cultivating a kind of felt sense that eventually allows the student to become capable of progressing without continually being taught.

The emphasis is not on teaching a large number of techniques or accumulating knowledge.

Instead, the student is encouraged to concentrate almost entirely, at least initially, on grasping the single most important and highest-priority sense — the thing that cannot be compromised.

Once that core sense is established, the student can supposedly continue improving spontaneously within everyday life.

The original writing also emphasizes temporarily letting go of everything learned in the past.

This description is not my own original wording. It is the starting point for the present analysis.

The questions explored here are:

* Why might this type of teaching work?
* Why is the initial stage so difficult for many people to sustain?
* Why is the initial investment difficult to evaluate rationally in advance?
* Why can everyday life itself eventually become a form of practice?
* Why can people who appear to be doing similar things end up with radically different results?
* And why does this structure seem to connect naturally with religious ideas such as surrender, letting go, and entrusting oneself to something?[https://medium.com/media/e2731096c86a7e7ceffb6ce26c4373aa/href](https://medium.com/media/e2731096c86a7e7ceffb6ce26c4373aa/href)

### 2. What Is Actually Being Taught Is Not Just a Technique

The common feature may not simply be that these teachers are teaching techniques in a particular way.

They may be trying to cultivate something more fundamental:

**an internal criterion by which the student can recognize and correct their own state.**

In ordinary learning, the structure often looks something like:

Teacher gives an instruction.

↓

Student understands it.

↓

Student practices.

↓

Teacher corrects the student.

↓

Student practices again.

In the teaching style described above, the teacher’s role may be somewhat different.

Instead of continuously supplying answers, the teacher helps the student acquire a criterion that allows them to recognize the answer through their own experience.

For example:

In martial arts, knowing the form of a technique is different from being able to feel whether the technique is actually functioning correctly within one’s own body.

In bodywork, knowing which body part to manipulate is different from being able to perceive the body’s actual state.

In meditation, knowing how a particular state is described is different from being able to recognize whether one is moving toward that state or unnecessarily interfering with the process.

Thus, what is being cultivated is not merely technical knowledge.

It is an internal feedback mechanism.

Once that mechanism becomes reliable, the student can continue learning even when the teacher is absent.

### 3. The Core Sense Becomes a Self-Correction Mechanism

The important point is that the core sense is not merely an answer.

It becomes a mechanism for self-correction.

Instead of asking:

> *“What is the correct thing to do?”*

the student gradually becomes able to ask:

> *“What is happening right now, and am I moving toward or away from the essential condition?”*

That is a fundamentally different learning structure.

The student no longer has to memorize every possible correct response.

They can use their own internal feedback to adjust.

This makes it possible for learning to continue outside formal practice sessions.

The teacher’s involvement can therefore decrease over time.

Paradoxically, a good teaching system may be one that makes the teacher progressively less necessary.

### 4. The Problem: The Initial Work Is Extremely Boring

The biggest difficulty with this approach is that the initial process is often extraordinarily mundane.

The actual task may not be complicated.

It may involve repeatedly doing something very simple.

Feel a subtle difference.

Repeat.

Do not understand it.

Repeat again.

Feel that something has changed slightly.

Repeat again.

From the outside, it may look as if almost nothing is happening.

There may be little visible progress.

There may be no obvious reward.

There may not even be a clear explanation of what the eventual result will look like.

And there is another problem:

**Nobody knows in advance how much time will be required.**

Is it 100 hours?

500?

2,000?

5,000?

The answer will vary between individuals, and even the definition of “having acquired it” may not be clear beforehand.

This makes the initial investment extremely difficult to evaluate rationally.

### 5. Why the Initial Investment Is Difficult to Rationalize in Advance

In my own case, I felt that it took roughly 2,000 hours of concentrated repetition before I had developed a sufficiently stable foundation that I was much less likely to misrecognize the core of the practice.

This number should not be interpreted as a universal prescription.

It does not mean that everyone needs 2,000 hours.

It does not mean that 2,000 hours guarantees a particular result.

And I did not begin with a rational calculation that investing 2,000 hours would eventually produce a certain outcome.

That is precisely the important point.

If I had known in advance:

> *“Invest 2,000 hours now, and afterward a large part of your everyday life will itself become practice,”*

then the investment would have been relatively easy to justify.

But before making the investment, none of that was certain.

I could not know:

* whether the method would really work,
* whether it would work for me,
* how much time it would require,
* whether another method might be more efficient,
* when meaningful results would appear,
* or even exactly what “success” would mean.

Therefore, the initial investment has an unusual structure:

**A large amount of time must be invested before the future return can be properly understood.**

### 6. In My Case, I Was Simply Lucky Enough to Feel Some Value in It

I do not think I was naturally the type of person who was especially suited to this kind of learning.

In fact, I tend toward the opposite.

I am inclined to regard my own felt sense as authoritative, and I have historically been extremely prone to doing things my own way.

In that sense, I would not describe myself as someone who naturally excels at unquestioningly following a teaching for a long period of time.

Yet the process happened to work.

I happened to experience something valuable in the practice itself.

That small sense of “this is good” was enough to allow the repetition to continue.

I did not have a clear rational forecast of the eventual outcome.

I did not know that the process would eventually become integrated into everyday life.

I simply had enough immediate experiential value to keep going.

Looking backward, a very large amount of time had accumulated.

So I currently regard myself less as someone who succeeded because of exceptional discipline or talent and more as someone who was **lucky enough for the conditions necessary for sustained practice to emerge.**

### 7. Why Many People Cannot Continue the Boring Work

It is tempting to explain the inability to continue such work as a lack of willpower.

But there may be a more structural explanation.

Most people have some degree of spare capacity.

They can continue living with their existing methods.

They can try something else.

They can acquire new information.

They can change approaches.

They can stop if the results do not seem convincing.

In other words:

**They have enough room to remain on the trajectory they were already following.**

That makes it difficult to justify fixing a large amount of resources onto one extremely narrow and apparently unrewarding task.

If the task does not immediately make sense, they can switch.

If another approach seems more efficient, they can try it.

If they become uncertain, they can return to what they already know.

This means that the problem may not simply be an inability to work hard.

It may be that there is no compelling reason to leave the existing trajectory.

### 8. Having Spare Capacity May Actually Make This Harder

This creates an apparent paradox.

Having more resources and more options is normally considered advantageous.

But when the goal is to develop an unfamiliar internal sense through long-term repetition, having many options may make the process harder.

If alternatives are always available, one can:

* try another method,
* return to an old method,
* modify the practice according to one’s own ideas,
* stop when progress is unclear,
* or constantly reevaluate whether the current approach is worthwhile.

Each of these choices may be reasonable.

But together, they prevent resources from becoming concentrated in one place for a sufficiently long period.

By contrast, someone who has no alternative may simply continue.

They may not possess greater willpower.

They may simply have fewer opportunities to distribute their resources elsewhere.

In this sense, **“having no choice but to continue” can itself create a powerful learning condition.**

### 9. Possible Types of People Who Can Sustain the Initial Investment

Several types of people may be able to cross this initial barrier.

### 1. The lucky person

The necessary conditions happen to arise.

There is enough time.

The environment supports repetition.

The person happens to experience some immediate value.

Daily life allows the practice to continue.

No exceptional talent is necessarily required.

### 2. The person with an existing foundation

Someone has already been practicing continuously for a long time.

They already possess the habit and volume of practice.

However, the core element has not yet been fully refined.

In this case, the existing foundation can be redirected toward the essential point.

### 3. The person who can accept it without hesitation

Someone may have been close to people who already embody the ability.

Because of that direct exposure, they may be able to accept the instruction without endlessly questioning it.

Or they may simply possess a disposition that allows them to commit without much hesitation.

The important characteristic here may not be “believing the teacher is right.”

It may simply be the ability to avoid continually reopening the decision.

### 4. The person who has no other choice

Someone may be in a situation where abandoning the process is not a realistic option.

Again, this does not necessarily mean greater strength of character.

The environment itself removes alternatives.

As a result, resources remain concentrated.

### 10. What These Types Have in Common: Leaving the Existing Trajectory

Although these people arrive at the process through different routes, they share something important.

They somehow manage to move outside the trajectory established by their previous habits.

This does not necessarily mean making a dramatic life change.

It can be much smaller.

It means temporarily suspending the usual way of deciding:

> *“I already know how I would normally do this.”*

Instead:

> *“For now, I will follow this one criterion and see what happens.”*

That small deviation from the familiar trajectory can be surprisingly difficult.

The old trajectory has a reason for existing.

It has worked well enough to sustain one’s life.

It contains previous successes.

It contains accumulated knowledge.

Therefore, abandoning it requires giving up something that is already familiar and functional in exchange for something whose value is not yet clear.

### 11. Temporarily Letting Go of Previous Learning

This connects with the original statement that previous learning should temporarily be let go.

The problem with previous learning is not necessarily that it is wrong.

The problem is that it can become an overly strong interpretive framework.

A new bodily experience appears.

The mind immediately tries to classify it using existing knowledge.

“This must be the same thing I learned before.”

“This probably means I should activate this muscle.”

“This is probably the correct way to do it.”

The result is that the person may be trying to reproduce an interpretation rather than actually perceiving the new experience.

Temporarily letting go of previous learning can therefore be understood as:

**moving previous knowledge out of the position of absolute authority.**

It does not mean destroying or permanently rejecting that knowledge.

It means allowing direct experience to come first, with conceptual knowledge available afterward as a reference.

### 12. When Everyday Life Itself Becomes Practice

Initially, there is a clear distinction:

**Practice time = learning**

**Ordinary life = everything else**

But once the core sense becomes sufficiently stable, this distinction begins to dissolve.

Walking.

Sitting.

Standing.

Working.

Resting.

Using the body.

Doing ordinary daily activities.

All of these can become opportunities to receive feedback.

The person no longer needs to create a special practice environment every time.

The important change is therefore not simply:

**“Practice time increases.”**

It is:

**“The range of time that can function as practice expands.”**

Eventually, everyday life itself becomes a continuous feedback environment.

### 13. The Structural Change Around the 2,000-Hour Point

In my own experience, after roughly 2,000 hours of concentrated repetition, I began to feel that I had developed a foundation that made it much harder to fundamentally misrecognize the core of the practice.

Again, the number itself is not the important part.

The important part is what happened to the structure of time afterward.

Before the foundation was established:

**Practice time = practice**

**Everyday life = ordinary life**

Afterward:

**Practice continues during ordinary life.**

The distinction becomes increasingly blurred.

Walking, sitting, working, resting, and moving through daily activities can all provide ongoing feedback.

Therefore, the actual amount of committed time is much larger than the initial 2,000 hours.

The initial investment creates the conditions under which everyday life itself can become a continuing process of repetition and self-correction.

### 14. The Difference Between Repeating an Action and Learning Through It

Two people can perform exactly the same external behavior every day.

Both may walk every day.

Both may train every day.

Both may practice a particular posture.

But the internal learning process can be completely different.

For one person, walking is simply transportation.

For another, walking is also an opportunity to detect subtle bodily deviations and automatically adjust them.

From the outside, the behavior looks almost identical.

Internally, however, one person is simply repeating an action while the other is continuously receiving feedback from it.

Therefore:

**External repetition is not necessarily equivalent to learning.**

What matters is whether the repetition contains a functioning feedback loop.

Once the core sense is sufficiently established, ordinary activities can become part of that loop.

### 15. Why Similar-Looking Practice Can Produce Radically Different Results

This provides one possible explanation for a familiar phenomenon:

**Two people appear to be doing almost the same thing, yet their results are completely different.**

Both practice every day.

Both spend time using their bodies.

Both appear to follow the same method.

But one person may simply be repeating the external form.

The other may be continuously checking and adjusting themselves through an internal criterion.

The difference is almost invisible from the outside.

Over years, however, the accumulated difference can become enormous.

This suggests that measuring practice only by visible hours may be inadequate.

A more meaningful question may be:

**How much of a person’s daily life is actually functioning as learning?**

### 16. The Appropriate Amount of Time Cannot Be Known in Advance

Another important feature of this type of learning is that the appropriate amount of time is difficult to determine beforehand.

People differ in:

* prior foundation,
* environment,
* sensitivity to bodily feedback,
* ability to maintain attention,
* accumulated practice,
* and the point at which they consider the ability sufficiently established.

Therefore, a simple rule such as:

> *“Practice for X hours and you will acquire it”*

is unlikely to capture the actual process.

Instead, the process may look more like:

Practice a little.

↓

Notice some value.

↓

Continue.

↓

Begin to recognize something.

↓

Continue.

↓

Eventually notice:

> *“Wait — something has changed.”*

The future result does not necessarily need to be understood in advance.

A small present-moment reward can be enough to sustain the next stage.

### 17. The “Wait, What?” Moment

That moment of:

**“Wait, something is different.”**

may be particularly important.

At first, the person may simply experience something as slightly better.

Then gradually:

* something becomes more natural,
* a distinction becomes easier to perceive,
* a correction happens without deliberate effort,
* the same sense appears during ordinary life,
* or something that previously required conscious attention begins happening automatically.

Only afterward does the meaning of the earlier boring work become clear.

In this sense:

**The investment is not rationalized first and rewarded later.**

Instead:

**The result eventually makes the earlier investment understandable.**

This is one reason why rational cost-benefit analysis has difficulty evaluating certain forms of learning in advance.

### 18. The Connection to Surrender, Letting Go, and Entrusting

This structure also helps explain why such practices can easily become connected with religious concepts such as surrender, letting go, or entrusting oneself to something.

At the beginning:

* the required amount of time is unknown,
* the future result is unknown,
* the mechanism is not completely understood,
* and the person cannot fully evaluate whether the process will work.

Yet they continue.

In religious language, this can easily be described as:

**surrender, trust, letting go, or entrusting oneself.**

However, “letting go” here does not necessarily mean abandoning judgment or becoming passive.

It can instead mean:

**stopping the attempt to repeatedly evaluate and manipulate something that one is not yet capable of evaluating accurately.**

### 19. Letting Go Does Not Mean Doing Nothing

The phrase “letting go” can sound like giving up effort.

But within this framework, the opposite may be closer to the truth.

Letting go means reducing unnecessary intervention so that the necessary process can continue.

Constantly asking:

* “Is this really worthwhile?”
* “Is there a more efficient method?”
* “Am I doing it correctly?”
* “How many hours should I continue?”
* “When will I get results?”

consumes cognitive resources.

Those resources could instead be invested in actually performing and sensing the practice.

Thus:

**Letting go does not mean doing nothing.**

It can mean:

**reducing unnecessary manipulation and allowing the necessary repetition to continue.**

### 20. Surrender Does Not Necessarily Lead to Permanent Dependence

There is another interesting possibility.

Surrender does not necessarily have to mean remaining dependent on an external authority forever.

At first:

> *“I cannot yet recognize this myself, so I will trust the teacher or the method.”*

Later, after sufficient repetition:

> *“I can now recognize this directly myself.”*

At that point, dependence on the teacher can decrease.

The progression can therefore look like:

**Trust in another person**

↓

**Trust in the method**

↓

**Development of direct bodily recognition**

↓

**Trust in one’s own internal feedback**

↓

**Autonomy**

From this perspective, teaching someone how to become independent and temporarily entrusting oneself to a teacher are not necessarily contradictory.

Temporary surrender may actually function as a bridge toward autonomy.

### 21. Why Ordinary People May Remain on the Existing Trajectory

This leads back to the question of why people generally have difficulty leaving their existing trajectory.

It may not be because they lack discipline.

It may be because they have enough options.

They can continue living normally.

They can try something else.

They can stop.

They can return to familiar methods.

They can preserve their existing worldview.

There is no immediate necessity to abandon it.

Therefore:

**The very fact that alternatives are available can make deep commitment to an uncertain path less likely.**

By contrast, when a person has:

* no meaningful alternative,
* unusually strong immediate experiential feedback,
* an existing foundation,
* or unusually low hesitation,

the same long-term commitment can become possible.

This means that people who eventually make a dramatic transition may not have had a clearer vision of the future.

They may simply have continued following a small present-moment signal long enough for the future structure to reveal itself.

### 22. The Overall Structure

Putting the entire argument together:

A person encounters a core sense that appears to be fundamental.

The task of developing that sense is extremely simple but extremely boring.

The necessary investment is unknown.

The future return is unknown.

Therefore, the investment is difficult to rationally justify in advance.

Most people can remain on their existing trajectory because they have alternatives and sufficient room to do so.

Some people, however, manage to concentrate their resources on the one essential point.

The reason may be luck, an existing foundation, lack of hesitation, direct exposure to someone who already embodies the skill, or simply having no other choice.

Through repeated exposure, the person gradually becomes able to recognize the core sense accurately.

Once that happens, the person no longer needs to learn only during formal practice.

Everyday life itself becomes a source of feedback.

Walking, sitting, standing, working, resting, and moving through ordinary life all become opportunities for repetition and self-correction.

At that point:

**Practice becomes integrated into life.**

The distinction between “practice time” and “ordinary time” becomes increasingly weak.

As a result, the person’s actual cumulative learning time may become enormous even though their visible lifestyle appears completely ordinary.

### 23. The Central Question Is Not Talent or Effort Alone

The central issue may therefore not simply be:

**How talented is the person?**

or:

**How hard does the person work?**

A more fundamental question is:

**Can the person maintain a sufficiently large investment in a point whose value they cannot yet fully understand?**

And then:

**Can that point eventually become an internal criterion that functions automatically throughout everyday life?**

The initial investment is difficult precisely because its future value cannot be properly calculated beforehand.

Therefore, the conditions that allow the investment to continue may be more important than raw willpower.

In my own case, the important conditions were that I happened to experience some value in the practice and that I happened to be able to cultivate it continuously, repetitively, and for long periods within everyday life.

After roughly 2,000 hours, I felt that I had developed a sufficiently stable foundation that I was much less likely to fundamentally misrecognize the core of the practice.

From there, a substantial portion of everyday life itself began functioning as practice.

Thus, the actual commitment cannot be represented by “2,000 hours” alone.

The larger effect came from the fact that those initial 2,000 hours changed the structure of subsequent time.

### 24. From “Practicing” to “Living the Practice”

Ultimately, the goal may not be to become someone who knows a large number of techniques.

Nor is it necessarily to become someone who continually receives correct answers from a teacher.

It may instead be to become someone who can:

**receive feedback from their own body, recognize what is happening, correct themselves, and continue learning without external instruction.**

That requires a core sense.

Developing that sense may require temporarily suspending previous learning and habitual self-judgment.

It may require a long period of boring repetition whose eventual value cannot be predicted.

And if that process succeeds, everyday life itself can become practice.

The progression can therefore be described as:

**“Practicing”**

↓

**“Living while practicing”**

↓

**“Life itself becoming practice.”**

At that point, the person may no longer need to think of themselves as “doing practice.”

They are simply living.

But their ordinary life continues to provide the repetition, feedback, and self-correction that once required deliberate practice sessions.

### 25. Final Synthesis

The deepest point of this inquiry may be that the decisive factor in development is not simply talent or effort.

It may be the ability to make a sufficiently large and sufficiently long investment in a very small, very boring, initially uncertain point.

The initial investment is difficult because its value cannot be calculated in advance.

That is why people naturally tend to remain on the trajectory they already understand.

They have alternatives.

They have spare capacity.

They have previous knowledge.

They have familiar methods.

They can keep evaluating.

They can keep modifying.

They can keep searching.

But all of those possibilities can also prevent sufficient concentration.

The person who somehow leaves that trajectory can accumulate a different kind of learning.

The core sense becomes more stable.

The internal feedback mechanism becomes more reliable.

The need for external instruction decreases.

And eventually, the distinction between practice and ordinary life begins to disappear.

The most interesting transformation is therefore not simply:

**“I became better at practicing.”**

It is:

**“The conditions that once required deliberate practice became embedded in ordinary life.”**

The initial investment creates the foundation.

The foundation turns everyday life into feedback.

The feedback turns everyday life into continued practice.

Continued practice produces greater autonomy.

And autonomy eventually makes the original teacher increasingly unnecessary.

This may also explain why concepts such as surrender, letting go, and entrusting oneself appear so naturally in religious and contemplative traditions.

At the beginning, one may have to temporarily relinquish the demand to understand everything in advance.

But the purpose of that surrender does not necessarily have to be permanent dependence.

It can be the temporary suspension of excessive self-interference until direct internal recognition becomes possible.

The trajectory can therefore be understood as:

**External guidance → concentrated repetition → internal recognition → everyday feedback → autonomous learning.**

Or, in more experiential terms:

**“Being taught” → “practicing” → “recognizing” → “living” → “no longer needing to be taught.”**

The deepest investment may therefore not be the accumulation of techniques.

It may be the cultivation of a single internal criterion that is reliable enough to turn ordinary life itself into a continuous learning environment.

### Tension Release, Bodily Integration, Rest, Wakefulness, and Sleep — Can Deep Relaxation and High Activation Coexist?

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*AwTI0wPjHmS_J63AZKih6Q.png)

### 0. Position of This Exploration

This document integrates a series of first-person observations concerning:

* deliberately releasing bodily and mental tension
* internal bodily integration
* deep relaxation
* wakefulness and subjective activation
* cognitive clarity
* physical activity
* sleep
* and the possibility of reducing sleep requirements

The terms used here — “activation,” “ease,” “mental clarity,” “bodily integration,” “qi-like sensation,” and “maximum relaxation” — refer to subjective and embodied experiences.

They should therefore be treated as a phenomenological model for further observation, not as proof of any particular physiological mechanism.

### 1. The Starting Point: Questioning the Simple Model of Relaxation

A common intuitive model is:

> *Release tension
→ relax
→ parasympathetic/rest state
→ sleepiness*

The person initially experienced something close to this.

When deliberately relaxing the body — sometimes simply allowing it to become limp, and sometimes intensifying relaxation during exhalation — the experience could become:

* strongly restful
* increasingly parasympathetic-like
* increasingly detached from active wakefulness
* almost as if the support structure of consciousness were disappearing
* close to falling asleep

In this mode, relaxation and sleepiness were closely coupled.

Thus, “releasing tension” initially functioned primarily as a tool for resting or falling asleep.

However, later observations revealed that this was only one possible outcome of the same general operation.

### 2. “Releasing Tension” Is Not a Single State

The person can now deliberately perform an internal operation that feels like “releasing tension” more or less at will.

However, there appear to be several distinct forms of this operation.

At least two can be distinguished.

### A. Maximum Relaxation / Sleep-Oriented State

Characteristics include:

* allowing the body to become very loose
* relaxation becoming stronger during exhalation
* a strong sense of rest
* a feeling that the support structure of consciousness is disappearing
* approaching the point of falling asleep
* potentially producing a feeling that could be frightening for some people because consciousness seems to be losing its foothold

This was historically used mainly for:

> *resting or falling asleep.*

It is therefore closely associated with the sleep transition.

### B. Bodily Integration / Deep Structural Tension

There is another state in which superficial tension is extremely low, while the body nevertheless feels internally organized and structurally integrated.

Characteristics include:

* breathing becoming almost motionless
* little or no deliberate respiratory manipulation
* very little superficial tension
* a sense that the body is standing or organizing itself beautifully through deeper layers
* a strong sense of internal bodily integration
* a sensation somewhat resembling “qi”
* the sensation becoming somewhat stronger during exhalation
* stronger relaxation tending to intensify the sensation

This is not the same as simply becoming limp.

Rather:

> *unnecessary superficial tension is released, while deeper structural organization remains intact.*

The body is not collapsing into rest.

It is highly integrated while remaining relaxed.

### 3. These Two States Had Not Previously Been Combined Very Much

Until recently, the person had generally used these two processes separately.

In particular:

* breathing was rarely used consciously anymore
* when it was used, it was usually very weak
* maximum relaxation was mainly used for sleep or rest
* there had not been a strong regular exercise habit
* there was little intention to combine maximum relaxation with bodily integration

Thus, the two processes existed independently:

> *bodily integration*

and

> *maximum relaxation.*

But the possibility of combining them had not been explored very much.

The recent discovery may therefore have resulted from combining:

> ***Bodily integration + maximum relaxation.***

### 4. Maximum Relaxation Alone Tends Toward Sleep

When the maximum-relaxation state is used by itself, the trajectory tends to be:

> *release tension
→ deeper relaxation
→ loss of the normal foothold of consciousness
→ drowsiness / drifting toward sleep*

This is consistent with the person’s previous experience.

Therefore, the earlier understanding that:

> *“Maximum relaxation makes me sleepy”*

was not wrong.

It may simply have described only one configuration of relaxation.

### 5. Bodily Integration Alone Does Not Necessarily Mean Deep Rest

The bodily-integration state is different.

It involves:

* clean structural organization
* deeper internal tension
* a sense of bodily unity
* a qi-like sensation

But it does not necessarily mean maximum rest.

Maintaining structural integration and entering a maximally restful state may be separate processes.

### 6. The Central Discovery: Bodily Integration + Maximum Relaxation

The new possibility is:

> *Establish bodily integration
+
layer maximum relaxation over it*

In other words:

> *the body remains deeply integrated at a structural level
+
superficial and conscious tension becomes extremely low
+
breathing becomes almost motionless
+
relaxation becomes deep enough that even the normal foothold of consciousness begins to disappear*

This combination appears capable of producing an unusually high degree of mental clarity.

That outcome had not previously been expected.

### 7. The Apparent Paradox: Maximum Relaxation and Maximum Vitality

This is the most important observation.

The intuitive model is:

> *deeper relaxation
→ lower activation
→ sleepiness*

But the current experience suggests another possibility:

> *deep relaxation
+
bodily integration
→ very high subjective activation
+
extreme physical ease
+
exceptionally clear cognition
+
intense cerebral wakefulness*

In other words:

> ***Deep relaxation and high activation may not necessarily be opposites.***

The “activation” described here should not be interpreted simply as nervous excitation or heightened tension.

It is closer to:

* the body feeling extremely easy to move
* very little unnecessary effort
* high physical vitality
* extremely clear cognition
* a strong sense of being fully awake

The state is therefore better described as:

> ***high vitality without unnecessary tension***

rather than simply “high arousal.”

### 8. Mental Clarity Appears to Have Levels

The person does not experience “mental clarity” as a single binary state.

As a subjective scale:

* previous ordinary state: roughly 30–60
* current ordinary baseline: consistently above roughly 70
* the best-feeling awakening ever experienced: above roughly 90
* the newly observed state: potentially beyond that 90+ level

These numbers are not physiological measurements.

They are simply a subjective framework for comparing different levels of clarity.

This distinction matters because the person’s current baseline is already unusually clear.

Therefore, the new state is not merely:

> *“I am not sleepy.”*

It may represent:

> ***a further increase in clarity above an already high baseline.***

For someone starting from a much lower baseline, the same transition might be experienced as an extraordinarily intense awakening.

### 9. Cognitive Clarity vs. Felt Cerebral Clarity

The current experience seems to contain at least two different forms of “clarity.”

### Cognitive / Perceptual Clarity

This includes:

* clear perception
* low cognitive noise
* clear thinking
* stable attention
* clean processing
* absence of mental fog

### Felt Cerebral Clarity

This is more directly phenomenological.

It resembles:

> *“The brain itself feels completely awake.”*

Characteristics include:

* a vivid sense of being fully awake
* something resembling awakening from extremely deep sleep
* no desire for a second sleep
* a feeling that the brain itself is exceptionally clear
* intense cerebral vitality

The newly observed state seems to combine:

> ***cognitive clarity + felt cerebral clarity.***

### 10. Why “Complete Relaxation” Is a Misleading Term

The phrase “complete relaxation” can easily cause misunderstanding.

It may imply:

* every muscle becoming completely limp
* the body collapsing
* no structural tension
* inactivity

But that is not what is being described.

There is still deep internal organization and structural tension.

A more accurate description would be:

> ***Removing unnecessary tension while preserving deep bodily integration.***

Or:

> ***Maintaining structural bodily integration while reducing superficial and conscious tension as far as possible.***

The important distinction is:

> *relaxed does not necessarily mean structurally collapsed.*

### 11. The Role of Physical Activity

The discovery became particularly clear after substantial physical activity.

The person normally spends a significant amount of time in relatively low-activity conditions, so the relationship between activity and this state had not been obvious.

Then, after spending many minutes performing physically demanding activity outdoors under intense summer heat — something that would generally be considered exhausting — the person became much more aware of the state.

Afterward, instead of simply feeling more fatigued, something unexpected occurred.

The person experienced:

* greater physical ease
* greater bodily activation
* easier movement
* stronger cerebral vitality
* greater overall subjective comfort

Most strikingly:

> ***the body felt even easier than it had when simply resting without exercise.***

This was not merely a reduction in perceived fatigue.

It felt like a transition into a qualitatively better state.

### 12. Activity May Not Simply “Create Fatigue”

The ordinary model is:

> *activity
→ fatigue
→ rest
→ recovery*

The observed experience suggests another possible sequence:

> *physical activity
→ change in bodily state
→ bodily integration
→ maximum relaxation
→ increased physical ease + activation + cerebral clarity*

This creates a possible phenomenological connection with the idea expressed by Daisuke Hori, known for advocating approaches to short sleep, that physical activity can contribute to removing fatigue.

The present observation does not establish that his broader theory is physiologically correct.

Nor does it mean that intense activity — especially in extreme heat — should be used as a fatigue-recovery method.

A more cautious interpretation is:

> ***Physical activity may alter the body’s state in a way that makes a particular deeply relaxed, highly integrated state easier to enter afterward.***

### 13. Physical Fatigue and Low Activation May Be Different Phenomena

The word “fatigue” may conceal several different experiences.

These could include:

* actual physiological fatigue from activity
* low activation from prolonged inactivity
* bodily sluggishness
* poor movement integration
* accumulated physiological fatigue
* subjective heaviness
* mental dullness

All of these may sometimes be described simply as:

> *“I feel tired.”*

The current experience raises the possibility that activity did not literally “remove fatigue.”

Instead:

> *activity may have shifted the body away from a low-activation or stagnant state and made another state accessible.*

Distinguishing these possibilities will be important in future observation.

### 14. The Important Fact: The High-Activation State Does Not Prevent Sleep

The newly observed state is not simply a state of excessive arousal that makes sleep impossible.

The person can still sleep from this state.

That means:

> *extremely high subjective activation
+
deep relaxation
+
strong physical ease
+
ability to fall asleep*

can coexist.

This is important because it suggests:

> ***wakefulness and sleepability may not be simple opposites.***

The person does not have to become dull or exhausted in order to sleep.

The system appears capable of transitioning into sleep even from a state that subjectively feels extremely awake.

### 15. An Even Stranger Observation: Sleep Can Lower the State

Another important observation is that after sleeping, the subjective level of activation and ease is actually somewhat lower than it was in the unusual pre-sleep state.

The sequence is therefore not simply:

> *high activation
→ sleep
→ recovery
→ even higher activation*

Instead:

> *bodily integration + maximum relaxation
→ extremely high activation and ease
→ sleep
→ awakening
→ slightly lower subjective activation/ease*

This does **not** imply that sleep is unnecessary or that sleep failed to provide physiological recovery.

Sleep has functions that cannot be inferred from subjective activation alone.

However, it does challenge the assumption that:

> *“the state after sleep must be the maximum state of recovery.”*

At least on the subjective dimensions of activation and ease, that does not necessarily appear to be true.

### 16. This Leads to a Deeper Question: What Is Sleep?

At this point, the question changes.

It is no longer simply:

> *“How can I sleep less?”*

It becomes:

> ***“What exactly is sleep doing?”***

If a person can simultaneously experience:

* deep relaxation
* bodily integration
* high subjective activation
* high physical ease
* extreme cognitive clarity
* strong cerebral wakefulness
* and the ability to fall asleep normally

then the following simple model becomes insufficient:

> *wakefulness = activity
relaxation = stopping activity
sleep = the ultimate form of relaxation*

A different possibility is:

> ***Sleep is a distinct physiological state with functions of its own, while certain forms of restoration can occur during wakefulness.***

### 17. Rather Than “Replacing Sleep,” Decompose the Functions of Sleep

It may be tempting to ask:

> *“Can this state replace sleep?”*

But a more useful question is:

> ***“Which functions of sleep, if any, can be fulfilled while remaining awake in a deeply restful state?”***

Sleep involves multiple processes, including:

* sleep homeostasis
* memory consolidation
* neural regulation
* metabolic processes
* endocrine regulation
* immune regulation

Therefore, extreme subjective clarity and physical ease do not demonstrate that sleep has become unnecessary.

At the same time, if a deeply relaxed waking state can provide some functions normally associated with recovery, that would be important for understanding the relationship between wakefulness and sleep.

### 18. Reduced Sleepiness Is Not the Same as Reduced Sleep Requirement

This distinction is essential when discussing short sleep.

There is a difference between:

> *“I do not feel sleepy.”*

and:

> *“My biological requirement for sleep has actually decreased.”*

The current experience cannot establish the latter.

However, if the deeply relaxed/high-vitality state could eventually be maintained for long periods without producing negative consequences, while actual sleep duration naturally decreased and subsequent physical and cognitive functioning remained stable, then the relationship with sleep requirement would become a meaningful question.

For now, the appropriate approach is observation rather than deliberately restricting sleep.

### 19. Current Working Model

The observations can tentatively be organized into four states.

### State A — Maximum Relaxation Alone

> *release tension
→ deep relaxation
→ loss of the normal foothold of consciousness
→ drowsiness / drifting toward sleep*

### State B — Bodily Integration

> *release superficial tension
→ deeper structural integration remains
→ body organizes itself efficiently
→ internal integrated sensation / “qi-like” sensation*

### State C — Bodily Integration + Maximum Relaxation

> *establish bodily integration
+
add maximum relaxation
↓
extreme physical ease
+
high physical activation
+
cognitive clarity
+
felt cerebral clarity
+
ability to sleep if desired*

### State D — Activity → Integration + Maximum Relaxation

> *physical activity
→ change in bodily state
→ bodily integration
→ maximum relaxation
→ State C becomes especially pronounced*

In State D, the person experienced greater subjective ease than during ordinary passive rest.

### 20. Why This Was Not Discovered Earlier

Several factors may explain why this combination was not noticed sooner.

### 1. Maximum relaxation was primarily used for sleep

It was treated as:

> *“the state used to fall asleep.”*

Therefore, its potential relationship with wakefulness was not explored.

### 2. Conscious breathing had become minimal

The person had largely stopped deliberately manipulating breathing, using it only weakly when it occurred at all.

This reduced opportunities to intentionally access the strongest form of relaxation associated with exhalation.

### 3. There was no strong exercise habit

The conditions under which activity followed by bodily integration and maximum relaxation could be observed were relatively uncommon.

### 4. There was little intention to combine the two processes

The two states were already familiar individually.

But the question:

> *“What happens if bodily integration is maintained while maximum relaxation is added?”*

had not been actively pursued.

### 21. A Conceptual Shift

The observations suggest reconsidering several assumptions.

### Assumption 1

“More relaxation necessarily means lower activation.”

→ Not necessarily.

### Assumption 2

“High wakefulness requires high bodily tension.”

→ High wakefulness and high tension may be separable.

### Assumption 3

“Rest means stopping activity.”

→ Activity may sometimes facilitate access to a state that feels more restorative than passive inactivity.

### Assumption 4

“If someone is relaxed enough to sleep, their activation must be low.”

→ Very high subjective activation may coexist with the ability to fall asleep.

### Assumption 5

“The state after sleep is necessarily the highest state of recovery.”

→ At least on subjective dimensions such as activation and ease, this does not necessarily appear to be true.

### 22. Future Observation

Rather than immediately attempting to reduce sleep duration, the first step is to characterize the states themselves.

Important variables include:

1. What happens with maximum relaxation alone?
2. What happens with bodily integration alone?
3. What happens when both are established simultaneously?
4. How does the state differ before and after physical activity?
5. Does the amount of activity matter?
6. Does the type of activity matter?
7. How long can the high-vitality state be maintained?
8. How easily can sleep be initiated from that state?
9. How does subjective activation change after sleep?
10. What happens to physical condition the following day?
11. What happens to cognitive performance?
12. Does actual sleep duration naturally change over time?
13. Does subjective clarity correspond to objective performance?
14. Does the state affect physiological markers of recovery or sleep pressure?

It is particularly important not to collapse the following into one variable:

> *absence of sleepiness
physical ease
high activation
cognitive clarity
cerebral vitality*

They should be observed separately.

### 23. Central Working Hypothesis

The most important hypothesis at present is:

> ***Wakefulness and relaxation may not be simple opposites.***

More specifically:

> ***It may be possible to reduce superficial, unnecessary tension while preserving deep bodily integration, allowing the body to enter a deeply restful state without losing high subjective activation and cognitive clarity.***

Furthermore:

> ***Physical activity may not merely increase fatigue; under certain conditions, it may alter the bodily state in a way that facilitates entry into this deeply relaxed, highly integrated state afterward.***

And finally:

> ***Sleep may not simply be “the opposite of wakefulness” or the final stage of relaxation. It may be a distinct physiological state with specific functions, while certain forms of restoration can occur during wakefulness.***

### 24. The Deeper Question

The ultimate question is no longer simply:

> *“Can I sleep less?”*

It is:

> ***How far can human beings simultaneously maintain deep rest and high wakefulness?***

And beyond that:

> ***Is sleep necessary primarily because the body becomes fatigued, or because sleep performs specific physiological processes that cannot be fully reproduced during wakefulness?***

If some functions normally associated with recovery can occur during a deeply relaxed waking state, then short sleep should not be conceptualized merely as:

> *“reducing the amount of sleep.”*

Instead, it could become a question of:

> ***reconstructing the relationship between rest, wakefulness, and sleep.***

However, none of the present observations establish that sleep can be replaced or that sleep requirements have actually been reduced.

The more productive next step is to investigate the relationships among:

> *maximum relaxation
bodily integration
high activation
subjective ease
cognitive clarity
felt cerebral vitality
physical activity
and sleep requirement*

as separate variables.

The broader possibility emerging from these observations is that what is ordinarily grouped together as “being awake,” “resting,” “being active,” and “sleeping” may actually consist of several partially independent dimensions.

The discovery is therefore not simply that “relaxation can make the brain feel more awake.”

It is that:

> ***the relationship between relaxation, activation, bodily integration, recovery, and sleep may be far less linear than ordinary experience suggests.***

# What Does It Mean to Experience the World as Real?
* **File**: 08_what_does_it_mean_to_experience_the_world_as_real.md | **Date**: Sun, 23 Aug 2026 01:37:09 GMT
* **URL**: [https://medium.com/@sato-kazuki/what-does-it-mean-to-experience-the-world-as-real-045b95f19ccd?source=rss-c95e8770f8e0------2](https://medium.com/@sato-kazuki/what-does-it-mean-to-experience-the-world-as-real-045b95f19ccd?source=rss-c95e8770f8e0------2)

### What Does It Mean to Experience the World as Real? — Non-Immersion, Brain-Feel, and Embodied Reality

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*o71QyGU8BHCVV35Gcy6gYw.png)

### 0. Starting Point

There is a common description of awakening experiences in which the world becomes more vivid, higher-resolution, more beautiful, or more intensely real.

However, this explanation tends to treat increased perceptual resolution and increased reality as if they were essentially the same thing.

They may actually be separate phenomena.

An increase in the amount of information entering conscious experience does not necessarily mean that the information itself possesses qualities such as “real,” “beautiful,” or “visceral.”

Instead, the experience of a “real world” may emerge from a combination of:

* the information coming from the external world
* how much of that information becomes subjectively available
* the degree of immersion
* emotional and bodily sensations
* self-relevance
* brain/body states reproduced from memory
* the subjective qualities of “real,” “beautiful,” “visceral,” or “present”

The central question is therefore not simply how much information is perceived, but how human beings transform information into an experience of reality.
[https://medium.com/media/b05e477bc4aa39e48ea974c6460becf1/href](https://medium.com/media/b05e477bc4aa39e48ea974c6460becf1/href)

### 1. The Amount of Information and the Sense of Reality Are Different Variables

Humans presumably receive an enormous amount of sensory information.

Vision alone contains information about:

* color
* brightness
* depth
* peripheral vision
* movement
* shape
* texture

and many other dimensions simultaneously.

Yet not all of this information explicitly appears in subjective experience.

When walking through a city, for example, information about buildings, people, sounds, air, light, body position, and spatial relationships may be continuously entering the nervous system.

But one does not normally experience each of these as separate pieces of information.

Instead, they are compressed into something like:

“I am walking through a city.”

Therefore:

**More information ≠ more reality.**

And:

**Less information ≠ less reality.**

Information quantity and subjective reality should be treated as separate dimensions.

### 2. Ordinary Consciousness May Be Immersive by Default

Most people probably do not notice that they are immersed in their ordinary perception.

The reason may be that immersion itself constitutes the default way the world is experienced.

A possible structure is:

External world
↓
Massive sensory input
↓
Natural selection through attention, prediction, value, and emotion
↓
Compression of what becomes subjectively explicit
↓
A unified experience of “being in the world”
↓
Emotional, bodily, and self-related sensations become attached
↓
The result is experienced as “the real world”

In this condition, an enormous amount of information may be entering the nervous system, while only a relatively small portion appears explicitly in subjective awareness.

### 3. What Changes When Immersion Decreases?

In a less immersive state, information that would normally remain in the background may become subjectively available.

For example:

* fine details within the visual field
* spatial location of sounds
* spatial structure
* relationships between body and environment
* peripheral visual changes
* texture
* subtle movements

may become more noticeable simultaneously.

Subjectively, this may be described as:

“The resolution of the world increased.”

“The world became much more detailed.”

However, this alone does not necessarily produce a stronger sense of reality.

This distinction is crucial.

### 4. The Hypothesis That “Reality” Is Not an Attribute of Information

One possibility is that “realness” is not an attribute inherently contained in external information.

A sunset has physical optical properties.

But subjective qualities such as:

* beautiful
* visceral
* moving
* nostalgic
* authentic
* intensely real

are not identical to those physical properties.

A more useful model may therefore be:

World
↓
Perceived information
↓
Neural and bodily integration
↓
Emotion, value, and self-relevance
↓
A particular subjective state
↓
The experience of “real,” “beautiful,” or “visceral”

In other words:

**World → reality**

may be less accurate than:

**World → information processing → brain/body state → experienced reality**

### 5. The Concept of “Brain-Feel”

A useful informal concept here is “brain-feel.”

“Brain-feel” is not a formal neuroscience term.

It refers to the subjective brain/body state associated with experiences such as:

* feeling that something is real
* feeling that something is beautiful
* feeling that something is vivid or visceral
* feeling that one exists
* feeling intensely present

The hypothesis is that these brain/body states may transform otherwise neutral sensory information into a particular subjective quality of reality.

The important point is that the brain-feel itself is not the external information.

### 6. The Model of “Information × Brain-Feel”

The previous ideas can be combined into a model for the overwhelming sense of reality sometimes reported during awakening experiences.

In ordinary consciousness:

Massive sensory input
↓
Immersive compression
↓
A relatively small portion becomes subjectively explicit
↓
Brain-feel, emotion, and bodily sensation become attached
↓
The world is experienced as real

In a non-immersive awakening state:

Massive sensory input
↓
Reduced compression
↓
A much larger amount of information becomes subjectively explicit
↓
Brain-feel, emotion, and bodily sensation become attached across a broader range of information
↓
The world may be experienced as extraordinarily vivid or real

Thus:

It may not be that increased information itself creates reality.

Rather:

**Increased information + a mechanism that generates subjective reality**

may produce an overwhelming sense of reality.

### 7. The Beauty and Vividness of Awakening Experiences Are Also Subjective Constructions

When someone in an awakening state reports:

“The world is unbelievably beautiful.”

“Everything is incredibly vivid.”

“The world feels intensely real.”

“The world feels alive.”

it does not necessarily mean that they have perceived the objective or ultimate nature of the world.

The experience of beauty, vividness, and reality is itself generated through that person’s brain, body, emotions, memories, and history.

The same external information may produce different subjective qualities in different people.

At the same time, human beings share many aspects of bodily and neural architecture, as well as cultural and developmental influences.

Therefore, people can still show substantial similarities in what they experience as beautiful, threatening, meaningful, or real.

But these common tendencies do not prove that “reality” is an intrinsic property of the information itself.

### 8. Reality as Something Specific to the Individual

“Reality” may emerge from the combination of:

**the information a person naturally selects or is naturally drawn toward**

and

**the brain/body state associated with that information.**

A possible structure is:

Information
↓
Natural attention and selection
↓
Certain information becomes grouped into a meaningful whole
↓
A particular brain-feel, bodily sensation, and emotion becomes attached
↓
That person’s experience of reality

This means that each person may, in a meaningful sense, experience a slightly different “real world.”

Human beings nevertheless share enough biology and experience that these realities overlap considerably.

### 9. The Hypothesis of Reproducing Childhood Reality Through Brain-Feel

If brain-feel can be reproduced through memory or related mechanisms, then it may be possible to reproduce aspects of the reality experienced during childhood.

For example:

A particular stimulus
↓
A particular bodily, emotional, and attentional state
↓
A particular brain-feel
↓
A particular way the world feels

may have become strongly associated during childhood.

As an adult, encountering the same stimulus may only produce a memory:

“That was something I liked when I was young.”

But if the brain-feel associated with the original experience can also be reproduced, then:

Current stimulus
+
Reproduced childhood brain-feel
↓
A reality resembling the childhood experience

might emerge.

In this model, “childhood reality” is not identical to the childhood world itself.

It is more like:

**Childhood world × childhood brain/body state**

Memory may therefore preserve not only information about past events, but also pathways for re-accessing something about how the world felt at that time.

### 10. Non-Immersion + Reproduced Brain-Feel Could Produce an “Extreme Reality”

Taking the model further, the combination of non-immersion and reproduced brain-feel could potentially create an unusually intense experience of reality.

Ordinarily:

Massive input
↓
Natural selection and compression
↓
A limited set of information
↓
Brain-feel
↓
Reality

Under non-immersion:

Massive input
↓
Reduced compression
↓
Large amounts of information
↓
Brain-feel applied across a much wider range
↓
Potentially overwhelming reality

This provides one possible interpretation of reports that awakening makes the world extraordinarily real.

The key factor may not simply be increased information.

It may be:

**Increased information × a broad application of a reality-generating brain/body state.**

### 11. Contrast With the Current State

In the current state being examined here, non-immersive perception and exposure to large amounts of information are relatively strong, while emotional and subjective brain-feel is greatly reduced.

The configuration can therefore be described approximately as:

* Information quantity: high
* Immersion: low
* Brain-feel: weak
* Bodily integration: preserved
* Emotional reality: weak
* Sense of unreality: largely absent

This does not produce the overwhelming reality sometimes described in awakening experiences.

Instead, it produces something closer to:

“There is a huge amount of information, but the information itself does not contain an intrinsic quality of reality.”

The world is still there.

Information is still there.

But additional subjective qualities such as:

“This is intensely real.”

“This is visceral.”

“This is beautiful.”

“This is profoundly alive.”

are much weaker.

The result is difficult to describe as either “real” or “unreal.”

It is closer to a state of calm or “nothingness.”

### 12. Lack of Reality Is Not the Same as Unreality

An important distinction is:

**Reduced reality-feel ≠ sense of unreality.**

If reality were treated as a simple line:

Real ←→ Unreal

the current experience would be difficult to describe.

It may be more useful to separate several dimensions:

* bodily/physical reality
* perceptual reality
* emotional reality
* brain-feel
* self-related reality
* sense of unreality

These dimensions can vary independently.

For example:

Bodily reality: strong
Perceptual information: high
Immersion: low
Emotional reality: weak
Sense of unreality: absent

This is possible.

It is therefore not necessarily a loss of contact with reality.

It may instead be a reduction in the subjective “reality-feel” layered onto otherwise intact perception and bodily integration.

### 13. Bodily Integration as a Foundation of Reality

One possible reason the current state does not become nihilistic or strongly unreal is that bodily integration remains sufficiently functional.

Examples include:

* feeling the feet contacting the floor
* sensing gravity
* knowing where the body is
* moving the body and receiving the expected feedback
* touching something and receiving tactile feedback
* maintaining a coherent relationship between body and environment

These physical and bodily feedback loops may constitute another layer of reality independent from emotional brain-feel.

Thus, even if emotional or subjective reality-feel becomes weak, the connection between body and physical world remains intact.

The result is not:

“The world does not exist.”

It is closer to:

“The additional subjective quality of reality is weak, while the physical relationship between body and world remains intact.”

### 14. Sense of Unreality May Be a Separate Brain-Feel

The sense of unreality may therefore not simply be what happens when ordinary reality-feel disappears.

It may involve a distinct subjective state:

“A state in which the world feels unreal.”

Thus:

**Absence of reality-feel ≠ sense of unreality.**

Psychedelic experiences provide an interesting conceptual example.

The external world does not simply disappear.

Instead, perception, integration, prediction, emotion, and bodily experience can change substantially, producing an experience in which:

“the nature of reality itself seems different.”

A possible model is:

World
↓
Altered perception/integration/emotional/bodily state
↓
A subjective quality of “unreality”

This suggests that unreality, like reality, may be a quality generated by the brain rather than a direct property of the external world.

This is a conceptual model rather than a complete neuroscientific explanation of psychedelic or dissociative states.

### 15. The Current Calm State Is Not Necessarily Nihilism

The current experience can be described as a weakening of reality-generating subjective qualities without necessarily producing nihilism.

It is not:

“There is no world.”

It is closer to:

“The process that normally gives the world a particular subjective texture has become weak.”

The world remains.

Information remains.

The body remains.

Interaction with the environment remains.

What is reduced is the additional subjective coloring:

“This is real.”

“This is beautiful.”

“This feels alive.”

“This matters to me.”

Thus, “nothing” does not necessarily mean existential emptiness.

It may mean that the usual subjective coloring has become extremely thin.

### 16. The Desire to Generate Reality Also Becomes Weaker

When reality is no longer generated automatically, the desire to restore or manufacture it may also become weaker.

This is important.

The sequence is not necessarily:

Reality-feel disappears
↓
I want reality-feel back
↓
I actively generate it

Instead:

Reality-feel disappears
↓
“It is not really a problem.”
↓
There is little motivation to deliberately recreate it

Yet there can still be a residual feeling:

“Even so, something is missing.”

This is not necessarily a strong desire or suffering.

It may simply be the detection of a difference between:

“How the world used to feel”

and

“How the world feels now.”

In that sense, it is less like wanting something and more like detecting a missing dimension.

### 17. Removing “Reality” Reveals the World Beneath It

One of the most interesting implications of this state is the possibility of asking:

“What remains if the subjective quality of reality is removed?”

Ordinary human experience tends to fuse:

world
+
reality-feel
+
emotion
+
self-relevance

into one seamless experience.

Therefore, it is difficult to imagine a world in which reality-feel has been stripped away while perception itself remains intact.

But when non-immersive perception and large amounts of information remain while emotional brain-feel becomes weak, the two can become distinguishable:

**Information about the world**

versus

**feeling that the world is real.**

This makes it possible to experience, at least conceptually:

“The information itself does not contain an intrinsic property called reality.”

### 18. A Multi-Dimensional Model of World Experience

A useful framework is to separate world experience into at least the following components.

### A. External information

Light, sound, touch, temperature, spatial relationships, movement, etc.

### B. Perceptual information quantity

How much of the incoming information becomes subjectively available.

### C. Immersion

How strongly perception is experienced as being “inside” the world.

### D. Attention and selection

Which information is naturally selected and which becomes background.

### E. Bodily integration

Body position, gravity, contact, movement, interoception, and physical interaction with the environment.

### F. Brain-feel

Subjective brain/body states associated with particular experiences.

### G. Emotion and value

Beautiful, frightening, pleasant, important, meaningful, etc.

### H. Self-relevance

How strongly information is connected to “me” or “my life.”

### I. Reality-feel

The subjective quality of something being real, vivid, present, or visceral.

### J. Sense of unreality

A distinct subjective quality in which something feels unreal, strange, or detached from ordinary reality.

These should not be assumed to be the same variable.

### 19. Overall Model

The phrase “the world feels real” may compress many different processes into one subjective label.

These can include:

* how much information is perceived
* how much of that information reaches conscious experience
* how immersive perception is
* how attention selects information
* how strongly the body is integrated with the environment
* emotional response
* self-relevance
* memory
* reproduced brain/body states
* the generation of subjective reality-feel

These components can vary independently.

Therefore, the statement:

“Awakening makes the world more real”

should be treated cautiously.

A better set of questions is:

* What information increased?
* What previously backgrounded information became explicit?
* Did immersion decrease?
* What changed in bodily experience?
* What emotional state accompanied the change?
* What kind of brain-feel was present?
* What exactly does “real” mean in that person’s report?

The current state is especially interesting because it separates these variables unusually clearly.

There is substantial non-immersion.

There is substantial information.

But the information itself does not appear to possess an intrinsic quality of reality.

Emotional brain-feel is weak.

Bodily integration remains intact.

There is little or no strong sense of unreality.

The result is neither simply “real” nor “unreal.”

It is closer to:

“The world is here. The information is here. But the additional subjective quality of reality is not strongly attached to it.”

From this perspective, the question changes.

The question is no longer:

**“How real is the world?”**

It becomes:

**“How does the human brain and body generate the subjective quality of reality?”**

And then an even deeper question follows:

**“What does the world look like when that subjective quality is reduced, while perception and bodily integration remain intact?”**

Reality may therefore be better understood not as a fixed property of the external world, but as a quality of experience generated through the way a human nervous system connects with that world.

### Returning Psychologicalized Vitality to the Body, An N=1 Exploration of Baseline Activation, Motivation, and Neuroplasticity

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*ZQl2JdJm2OtkXPI2Ytr9zg.png)

### 1. The Core Question

In ordinary language, being “energetic,” “lively,” or “full of vitality” is often closely associated with motivation, enthusiasm, excitement, positive emotion, or the desire to do something.

But are these things actually the same?

This exploration attempts to distinguish between:

1. **Activity driven by psychological motivation**, and
2. **A more fundamental bodily capacity for activity that exists independently of psychological motivation.**

The second is provisionally referred to here as **baseline activation**, **intrinsic bodily vitality**, or simply **“vitality.”**

This is not a medically established physiological category. It is a conceptual model based primarily on subjective observation and an N=1 experience.

The proposed baseline activation has characteristics such as:

* The body can remain highly capable of movement even when there is little desire to do anything.
* Physical activity can be initiated when necessary without requiring strong motivation.
* The body can remain internally active even while doing nothing.
* Activity does not require continuous emotional or motivational stimulation.
* A sense of comfort and whole-body integration can accompany this activation.[https://medium.com/media/e76083b9d0a9c0b09d763350d844fb47/href](https://medium.com/media/e76083b9d0a9c0b09d763350d844fb47/href)

### 2. Are “Vitality” and “Motivation” Actually Different?

Ordinarily, the process is often understood as:

Motivation
↓
Desire to act
↓
Physical activity
↓
“I feel energetic”

But perhaps this represents only one way in which activity is generated.

For example:

* I move because something is fun.
* I move because I want to.
* I move because I have a goal.
* I move because I am excited.
* I move because I feel emotionally stimulated.
* I move because I feel that I have to.

In these cases, psychological meaning and emotion function as fuel for physical activity.

When that psychological fuel decreases, activity may also decrease.

This can produce the conclusion:

### “I have no desire to do anything”

“I have no energy.”

But these two states may actually be separable.

### 3. Young Children as an Intuitive Model of Baseline Vitality

Young children provide an intuitive example of what baseline activation might look like.

Children do not necessarily need to consciously generate motivation before becoming active.

They suddenly run.

They play.

They jump.

They explore.

They become absorbed in something that interests them.

Then they stop.

They rest.

They sleep.

And later they become active again.

There is often no elaborate psychological process of:

“I need to motivate myself so that I can become energetic.”

Instead, the body seems to possess a substantial reserve of spontaneous activity, and environmental stimulation or interest simply directs that activity.

An important point is that a child can also spend considerable time doing nothing without necessarily being “low-energy.”

Therefore:

**Activity level and vitality are not necessarily the same thing.**

Doing nothing does not necessarily mean lacking vitality.

### 4. The Psychologicalization of Vitality in Adulthood

As humans mature, they acquire increasingly complex systems involving:

* goals
* social roles
* responsibilities
* evaluation
* meaning
* future prediction
* expectations
* rewards
* obligations

As a result, the following structure may become increasingly dominant:

Purpose
↓
Meaning
↓
Motivation
↓
Action

For the purposes of this model, this can be called the **psychologicalization of vitality**.

The psychological system increasingly manages and mobilizes physical activity.

Instead of:

Body → action

the system becomes more like:

Psychological meaning → motivation → body → action.

As a result, psychological states such as:

“I want to.”
“I should.”
“This is fun.”
“This is meaningful.”
“I want to succeed.”

become important triggers for physical activity.

When those psychological drivers weaken, the person may interpret the resulting reduction in activity as:

“I have lost my energy.”

However, the physical capacity itself may not actually have disappeared.

### 5. Psychologicalized Vitality vs. Intrinsic Bodily Activation

These two forms of vitality can be distinguished conceptually.

### Psychologicalized vitality

Psychological motivation is used as fuel.

For example:

Something is enjoyable
↓
I want to do it
↓
I become active.

Or:

There is a goal
↓
I have to do it
↓
I become active.

In this state, psychological fuel is closely coupled with activity.

### Intrinsic bodily activation

Psychological motivation is not required to maintain basic activity capacity.

The body is well integrated
↓
Baseline activity remains available
↓
A need, interest, or environmental demand appears
↓
Action can occur naturally.

In this state, there is no need to constantly want something.

It becomes possible to have:

“I do not particularly want to do anything, but if something needs to be done, I can simply do it.”

### 6. Activation Is Also Different from Nervous Arousal

Another important distinction is between **activation** and **nervous arousal**.

High arousal can produce experiences such as:

* wanting to move
* wanting to talk
* excitement
* restlessness
* strong desire to do something

But this does not necessarily represent the same thing as baseline vitality.

Arousal can increase activity, but it may also involve unnecessary muscular tension, emotional amplification, prediction, and cognitive effort.

Therefore:

**Nervous arousal ≠ motivation ≠ baseline activation ≠ physical capability.**

A person can have low psychological or nervous arousal while retaining substantial physical activity capacity.

This is particularly important in the current N=1 observation.

### 7. Current N=1 Observations

In the current state, it has become increasingly difficult to use cognition or emotion to “override” bodily states.

Previously, psychological states such as:

* excitement
* enjoyment
* anticipation
* emotional engagement
* a sense of obligation

could partially override or mask bodily sensations.

Psychological meaning could effectively persuade the body to keep going.

Now, this kind of psychological override appears much weaker.

At the same time:

* Relaxation itself is relatively easy.
* Physical movement remains highly available once movement actually begins.
* Physical activation may subjectively feel higher than before.
* There is little spontaneous desire to pursue activities.
* Desires may exist, but they rarely become strong enough to turn into impulses.
* Relaxation is often more attractive than acting on those desires.
* From the outside, this could easily look like “having little energy.”

This leads to an important hypothesis:

**The apparent lack of energy may sometimes be a lack of psychological drive rather than a lack of physical vitality.**

### 8. Reframing the Idea of “Cultivating Energy”

The ordinary idea of cultivating energy often involves:

* doing enjoyable things
* finding hobbies
* increasing motivation
* becoming more positive
* seeking stimulation
* social interaction
* pursuing rewarding experiences

These approaches essentially attempt to increase activity by modifying psychological conditions.

The present model suggests a different approach:

**Do not necessarily create more energy. Instead, allow the body’s existing activity capacity to become available without requiring psychological fuel.**

In other words:

Do not add activation.

Remove what is obstructing activation.

This distinction is central.

### 9. Returning Vitality to the Body

In the current subjective experience, several bodily regions appear particularly important as entry points into this state:

* sacrum
* pelvis
* pelvic floor
* lower abdomen
* lumbar region
* thoracic spine
* epigastric region
* cervical spine
* throat
* head

In particular, changes in the balance of the sacrum, pelvic floor, and lower abdomen can produce a striking sensation of the body’s superficial muscular tension “letting go.”

This can then be experienced as a continuous sense of release and integration extending through:

Sacrum / pelvis
↓
Lower abdomen
↓
Epigastric region
↓
Thorax
↓
Cervical spine
↓
Throat
↓
Head

This should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of a literal anatomical energy channel.

A more conservative interpretation is that multiple systems may be changing simultaneously, including:

* postural control
* coordination between deep and superficial muscles
* breathing
* intra-abdominal pressure
* coordination between pelvis and spine
* sensory input
* motor control
* autonomic regulation
* interoception
* attentional processes

The subjective experience of these coordinated changes may then appear as a single continuous “line” or “flow” through the body.

### 10. The Paradox: Less Tension, More Activation

Ordinarily, people may intuitively assume:

Low energy
↓
Put more effort into the body
↓
Increase activation.

The observed experience can be almost the opposite:

Excess superficial tension
↓
Reduced freedom of movement
↓
Less efficient whole-body coordination

Then:

Release unnecessary tension
↓
Improve coordination between deeper structures and the skeleton
↓
Improve breathing and postural organization
↓
Increase whole-body integration
↓
Increase subjective activation.

In this sense, activation may not need to be “added.”

It may emerge when unnecessary constraints are removed.

The practical principle becomes:

**Do not force activation. Remove what prevents activation from expressing itself.**

### 11. Whole-Body Integration and Intrinsic Comfort

Another important feature is that increased integration is not experienced merely as “having more energy.”

It can also produce a sense that:

**the whole body simply feels good without anything needing to happen.**

This differs from:

A stimulus feels good
↓
I want more stimulation
↓
I become active.

Instead:

The body becomes more integrated
↓
Internal resistance decreases
↓
The body itself becomes comfortable
↓
Activity remains readily available.

The source of comfort is therefore not necessarily external stimulation.

The bodily state itself becomes rewarding.

### 12. The Brain and Head-Related Component

The process does not appear to be purely peripheral.

Changes in attention or sensation around areas such as:

* the eyes
* behind the eyes
* the brow
* the head
* the cervical spine
* the throat

can also alter the subjective experience of the brain and whole-body state.

However, this should not be reduced to the idea that manipulating one point in the head simply “creates energy.”

There may instead be bidirectional interaction:

**Body → brain / perception**

and

**Brain / attention → body.**

For the specific question of baseline vitality, however, the spontaneous whole-body integration that emerges from the body may be more fundamental than consciously manipulating particular sensations.

### 13. A Neuroplasticity Hypothesis: Reallocating Activity Back to the Body

One possible way to conceptualize the long-term process is through neuroplasticity.

A hypothetical developmental trajectory could be:

Childhood
↓
Strong intrinsic bodily activity
↓
Natural movement and exploration
↓
Psychological meaning develops around those activities

Then adulthood:

Increasing psychological complexity
↓
More dependence on meaning, goals, evaluation, and motivation
↓
Psychological systems increasingly mobilize physical activity
↓
Intrinsic bodily activation becomes less consciously accessible or less dominant.

The proposed reverse process would be:

Release unnecessary tension
↓
Integrate posture, breathing, deep musculature, and bodily sensation
↓
Generate a coherent bodily state without relying on psychological motivation
↓
Experience spontaneous physical activation in that state
↓
Repeat
↓
The nervous system gradually learns the state
↓
The state becomes increasingly automatic.

In this model, “returning vitality to the body” means gradually making intrinsic bodily activation less dependent on psychological mobilization.

### 14. Easy to Perform Does Not Mean Quick to Consolidate

The actual technique may eventually feel simple:

Relax.

Align.

Integrate.

Allow the body to become coherent.

However, creating a state voluntarily and making that state the nervous system’s default are very different things.

If neuroplasticity is involved, long-term change may require:

* repetition
* time
* consistent exposure to the state
* repeated association with bodily sensation
* integration into ordinary daily activity

Therefore:

**A method can be simple while its consolidation takes a long time.**

The apparent simplicity of the practice should not be confused with the speed of nervous-system adaptation.

### 15. The Major Obstacle: Psychologicalized Vitality Consumes the Time Needed to Change It

This may be the most interesting paradox in the entire model.

If a person’s activity is strongly driven by psychological motivation, then spending time on a seemingly unproductive bodily process may feel difficult.

The person may think:

“Why am I doing this?”

“I could be doing something more enjoyable.”

“I need a concrete result.”

“Is this really worth my time?”

“Wouldn’t it be better to pursue my goals?”

The existing psychological system itself becomes an obstacle to developing a less psychologically dependent form of activation.

The irony is:

**The stronger the psychological motivation system is, the harder it may be to devote time to cultivating an alternative source of vitality.**

And because the existing system already works, there may be little perceived reason to change it.

### 16. A Self-Maintaining Motivation Loop

Psychologicalized vitality may form a self-reinforcing loop:

Psychological stimulus
↓
Motivation
↓
Activity
↓
Reward
↓
More psychological motivation
↓
More activity

This loop can be highly effective.

But when motivation decreases:

Reduced motivation
↓
Reduced activity
↓
“I have no energy”
↓
Search for psychological stimulation
↓
Temporary restoration of motivation.

This can reinforce the assumption that:

**Energy = psychological desire to act.**

The underlying bodily component may therefore remain largely unexplored.

### 17. Why the Current N=1 Situation May Be Unusual

The current condition contains several factors that make this process unusually accessible:

* Psychological desires are relatively weak.
* The pressure to constantly accomplish something is low.
* Relaxation itself is not perceived as a problem.
* Long periods can be spent observing and adjusting bodily states.
* Physical activation and psychological motivation have become relatively decoupled.

This creates an unusual opportunity:

**Time can be invested in bodily learning without requiring a strong psychological reason to do so.**

Therefore, the apparent simplicity of the process may partly reflect favorable conditions rather than the method being universally easy.

The relevant factor may not simply be:

“The technique is easy.”

It may be:

**“The psychological system is no longer strongly competing with the bodily learning process for time and attention.”**

### 18. Reinterpreting “If You Have Energy, You Can Do Anything”

The vitality described here is not high emotional intensity.

It is not constant enthusiasm.

It is not perpetual motivation.

It is closer to:

* being energetic even while doing nothing
* having little desire without becoming physically inert
* being able to act when action becomes necessary
* returning to rest without needing to “recover motivation”
* maintaining bodily activity without continuous stimulation

Thus:

Vitality
≠
Constant activity.

Instead:

**Vitality = freedom to alternate between activity and rest without losing underlying activity capacity.**

The important property is not the amount of activity.

It is the **autonomy of activity capacity**.

### 19. The Overall Hypothetical Model

The entire model can be summarized as follows.

### Childhood

Intrinsic bodily activity
↓
Natural exploration and movement
↓
Psychological meaning develops afterward

### Adulthood

Psychological meaning
↓
Motivation
↓
Physical activation
↓
The experience interpreted as “being energetic”

### Re-integration

Reduce unnecessary psychological and muscular effort
↓
Release bodily tension
↓
Integrate pelvis, spine, breathing, deep musculature, and sensory systems
↓
Experience intrinsic bodily activity without requiring strong motivation
↓
Repeat
↓
Neuroplastic adaptation
↓
The state becomes increasingly automatic
↓
Less dependence on psychological motivation
↓
Action becomes available when needed.

This does not mean literally “becoming a child again.”

The goal would instead be:

**To retain adult cognitive abilities, goals, intentions, and social capacities while rebuilding a body that does not require large amounts of psychological fuel in order to become active.**

The result would be a form of adult functioning in which psychological motivation directs activity rather than having to generate the underlying capacity for activity.

### 20. Questions for Further Investigation

This model raises several questions worth exploring:

1. How much of what people call “energy” is actually psychological motivation?
2. How physiologically separable are baseline activation and sympathetic or emotional arousal?
3. What is the relationship between whole-body integration and subjective physical vitality?
4. How do pelvic, spinal, respiratory, and deep-muscular coordination influence perceived activation?
5. Can repeated bodily-state practice produce durable neuroplastic changes?
6. If baseline activation becomes more stable, how does motivation itself change?
7. How should bodily vitality be distinguished from psychological desire?
8. Can “doing nothing” be clearly separated from “having no energy”?
9. When other people attempt to develop this kind of state, is the primary obstacle physical technique or psychological structure?
10. What effects might increased baseline activation have on cognition, movement, creativity, adaptability, and recovery?
11. Can a person maintain high baseline activation without simultaneously increasing nervous-system arousal?
12. Does reduced dependence on psychological motivation increase behavioral freedom rather than reduce it?

### 21. Central Hypothesis

The central hypothesis of this exploration is:

**Vitality may not fundamentally mean wanting to do something. It may mean retaining the bodily capacity to act naturally even when there is no particular desire to act.**

Adult development may involve progressively outsourcing much of this activity to psychological systems involving meaning, motivation, emotion, goals, and social expectations.

If so, one possible form of bodily practice is not to manufacture more motivation, but to gradually return some of this activity capacity to the body itself.

The process may involve:

* releasing unnecessary muscular tension
* improving whole-body coordination
* integrating pelvis and spine
* refining breathing and postural organization
* repeatedly experiencing bodily activation without psychological stimulation
* allowing the nervous system to learn this state through repetition.

The ultimate state is therefore not:

**“A person who always wants to do something.”**

It is closer to:

**“A person who can be completely at rest without losing vitality, and who can act naturally whenever action becomes necessary.”**

This is not high arousal.

It is not constant motivation.

It is not constant activity.

It is **autonomous availability of activity**.

In that sense, “vitality” may be better understood not as the amount of activity a person produces, but as the degree to which their capacity for activity is independent of psychological stimulation.

# Neuroplasticity of Tension Linked to Specific Cognitions, Focal Dystonia, Yips, Performance…

### Neuroplasticity of Tension Linked to Specific Cognitions, Focal Dystonia, Yips, Performance Tension, and Everyday Conditioned Responses

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*To2y20B2sNA7l5s4cKCcMQ.png)

### 1. The Basic Question

Focal dystonia, yips, excessive tension during sports or musical performance, and everyday experiences such as suddenly becoming physically tense in specific situations are not necessarily the same phenomenon.

They can involve different neurological and psychological mechanisms.

However, they may share a broader structure:

**A specific cognition, situation, or prediction can become associated with a specific bodily response through long-term learning.**

From this perspective, excessive tension is not necessarily just an unwanted physical reaction. It can potentially become part of an automated learned pattern.
[https://medium.com/media/0abc087f2e8e5f95914eb282794450ba/href](https://medium.com/media/0abc087f2e8e5f95914eb282794450ba/href)

### 2. The Basic Hypothesis: Tension Itself Can Be Learned

Normally, a movement might follow a relatively simple sequence:

**Recognize the movement → activate the motor program → move**

But if excessive force, muscular contraction, attentional monitoring, pressure, or physiological arousal repeatedly accompany that movement, another pattern may gradually develop:

**Recognize the movement → become tense → perform the movement**

The important point is that tension can potentially become **attached to the movement itself**.

For example, in piano playing:

> *“Move this finger”
↓
Excessive tension in the finger or hand
↓
Play the note*

If this combination is repeated over a long period, the relationship between the movement representation and the tension response may become increasingly strong.

The same principle could potentially appear in sports:

> *“Throw the ball in this situation”
↓
Excessive contraction of the arm or shoulder
↓
Throw*

Or in everyday life:

> *“Speak in front of people”
↓
Throat and body become tense
↓
Speak*

The situation itself can eventually become a trigger for the bodily response.

### 3. The Reaction Can Become Automatic

As a response becomes sufficiently learned, the person does not necessarily consciously decide to become tense before the body reacts.

Motor preparation begins before conscious awareness of the action is fully formed.

Therefore, subjectively, someone may experience:

> *“As soon as I tried to do it, my body locked up.”*

But the nervous system may already have begun preparing the body before the person consciously experiences the intention to act.

This creates an important distinction.

The problem may not simply be:

**“I consciously became tense.”**

It may instead be:

**“The recognition or prediction of the situation automatically triggered the tension response.”**

This also explains why simply telling someone:

> *“Don’t tense up.”*

may not be sufficient.

### 4. The Critical Point Is the Beginning of the Prediction

Rather than looking only at the muscles, it is useful to ask:

**What causes the tension response to begin?**

For example:

* playing the piano
* throwing a ball
* speaking in public
* talking to a particular person
* performing an important task
* entering a high-pressure situation

The trigger may be the recognition or prediction of the situation itself.

The structure becomes:

**Cognition → bodily response**

Once sufficiently automated, the bodily response can appear before the actual movement begins.

Therefore, the problem is not necessarily just muscular tension.

It can involve the interaction between:

* cognition
* prediction
* motor preparation
* sensory processing
* autonomic arousal
* muscular activity
* learned motor patterns

### 5. Neuroplasticity and Thresholds

Neuroplastic changes do not necessarily become consciously noticeable little by little.

A person may spend years:

* repeating the same movement
* using the body in the same way
* repeatedly applying excessive force
* monitoring the movement intensely
* performing under pressure
* practicing while fatigued

Eventually, a threshold may be crossed.

From the person’s perspective, this can feel like:

> *“I was completely fine yesterday, and suddenly I couldn’t do it today.”*

But the underlying changes may have accumulated gradually, with the visible symptom appearing only after a certain threshold was reached.

Thus, the apparent sudden onset does not necessarily mean that the underlying learning occurred suddenly.

### 6. The Problem of Repeating the Abnormal Pattern

Once an abnormal tension pattern has developed, continuing to perform the same movement while that pattern is active may provide additional opportunities to reinforce it.

This creates a potential problem:

> *“I can’t do it properly, so I need to practice even more.”*

More practice is not automatically better if the practice repeatedly reinforces the same dysfunctional pattern.

The important question becomes:

**What state is being practiced?**

Not merely:

**How many repetitions are being performed?**

This distinction is particularly important when thinking about focal dystonia and other movement disorders.

### 7. Focal Dystonia

Focal dystonia is a neurological movement disorder in which involuntary abnormal postures or movements occur, often in specific contexts or during specific actions.

In musicians, for example, symptoms may include finger curling, unwanted finger extension, or abnormal hand postures during particular playing patterns.

Focal dystonia involves neurological factors related to motor control, sensorimotor integration, and plasticity.

Therefore, it should not be reduced to:

> *“The person simply learned to become tense.”*

However, there is a potentially relevant overlap:

**A particular movement representation can become associated with abnormal muscle activity and abnormal motor patterns.**

The important distinction is that the present model describes a potentially shared learning principle; it does not claim that focal dystonia and ordinary tension are the same disorder.

### 8. Yips

Yips are also heterogeneous and may involve combinations of neurological, psychological, attentional, and situational factors.

However, a common feature can be the disruption of an otherwise highly automated skilled movement.

An experienced athlete normally does not consciously control every component of a well-learned movement.

But after a failure or period of pressure, attention may shift toward the movement:

> *“I have to make sure my wrist is positioned correctly.”
“I can’t fail this time.”
“I need to relax my hand.”*

The person begins consciously monitoring something that previously operated automatically.

This can potentially create a loop:

**Excessive monitoring**
↓
**Increased bodily tension**
↓
**Movement disruption**
↓
**Failure or near-failure**
↓
**Increased anticipation of failure**
↓
**More monitoring and tension**

This can make the next attempt even more difficult.

### 9. Everyday Situational Tension

The same mechanism should not be assumed to explain all everyday tension or anxiety.

However, at a more general level, many everyday reactions can be understood through the structure:

**Specific situation or cognition → learned bodily response**

Examples include:

* becoming tense only when speaking in public
* the throat becoming tight during phone calls
* the hands becoming tense during precision work
* the body becoming rigid when interacting with a particular person
* becoming physically tense when something “must not go wrong”
* suddenly losing natural movement in an important situation

These are not necessarily disorders.

They can simply represent learned, context-dependent physiological responses.

### 10. The Reverse-Learning Hypothesis

This is the central idea.

If:

**Specific cognition → tension**

can become reinforced through repetition, then perhaps:

**Specific cognition → relaxation**

can also be learned.

In other words:

**The same principle may work in the opposite direction.**

If repeated pairing of a particular situation with tension gradually makes tension automatic, then repeatedly entering that situation while maintaining a genuinely relaxed bodily state may create a competing learned association.

The goal is not necessarily to “fight” the tension.

It is to provide the nervous system with another possible response.

### 11. Doing the Opposite of What Was Learned

The basic idea can be stated very simply:

> ***If the same thing was learned in one direction, the opposite state can potentially be learned through the same principle.***

For example:

### Original pattern

**Specific situation**
↓
Tension
↓
Muscular contraction
↓
Action

### Alternative pattern

**Specific situation**
↓
Relaxed bodily state
↓
Action without unnecessary contraction

The important point is not a specific number of repetitions.

There is no simple rule such as:

> *“You practiced the old pattern 1,000 times, so you need to practice the new pattern 1,000 times.”*

Neuroplasticity does not work like a simple counter.

What matters is the **quality and state of the learning**.

### 12. Preventing Tension vs. Relaxing After Tension Appears

There is an important difference between:

**Tension → notice it → try to relax**

and:

**Recognize the situation → establish relaxation before the tension response begins → enter the situation in that state**

The second approach is fundamentally different.

It does not necessarily attempt to eliminate an already-established tension response.

Instead, it attempts to establish a different bodily state **before the learned tension response is activated**.

The subjective experience can therefore become:

> *“I know this situation normally makes me tense.”
↓
Establish the relaxed bodily sensation beforehand.
↓
Enter the situation.
↓
The expected tension never appears.*

This is different from successfully suppressing tension after it has already emerged.

### 13. The Importance of Actually Knowing the Sensation of Relaxation

Simply telling someone:

> *“Relax.”*

is not necessarily enough.

The person needs some actual bodily reference for what relaxation feels like.

Once someone can recognize and reproduce a genuinely relaxed state, they gain another option besides the familiar tension response.

Instead of:

> *“I have to stop myself from becoming tense.”*

the process becomes:

> *“I know what a non-tense state feels like, and I can enter this situation from there.”*

This changes the problem from **suppressing a response** to **expanding the available repertoire of bodily states**.

### 14. The User’s Personal Observation

In the present model, an important observation comes from personal experience.

The user has found that when a particular situation is approaching, introducing the sensation of relaxation beforehand can sometimes prevent the expected tension from appearing at all.

This is not experienced as:

> *“I became tense and then forced myself to relax.”*

It is closer to:

> *“I know this state is coming.”
↓
Apply the sensation of relaxation beforehand.
↓
The tension does not arise.*

This distinction is important.

The intervention is not necessarily about suppressing the reaction.

It may instead be changing the bodily state **before the reaction is triggered**.

The user also reports that this has generalized to a very large portion of everyday life rather than remaining limited to one particular movement.

That raises the possibility that the intervention is not merely correcting individual movements but may be changing the broader tendency to automatically generate excessive bodily tension in response to cognition and situations.

This remains an individual observation, however, and should not be generalized into a medical treatment claim.

### 15. “Most People Only Know How to Become Tense”

One particularly interesting part of this model is the possibility that many people simply do not have a practiced alternative.

When people become tense, they may know how to:

* try harder
* endure it
* concentrate more
* use willpower
* tell themselves to calm down
* take a deep breath
* force themselves through the situation

But these strategies do not necessarily amount to having a **well-developed bodily state that is genuinely different from tension**.

As a result, someone may effectively know only one way of operating in a particular situation:

**“This is how my body functions here: tense.”**

The problem is then not necessarily that the person lacks the ability to relax in general.

They may simply lack the learned ability to **remain relaxed while performing that particular action or entering that particular situation**.

### 16. Tension and Relaxation as Learnable States

This does not mean:

**Tension = bad**
**Relaxation = good**

Tension is an essential physiological function.

The problem arises when excessive tension is automatically recruited in situations where it is unnecessary.

Therefore, the ideal outcome is not necessarily to eliminate tension entirely.

It is to increase the ability to choose or access the appropriate bodily state for the situation.

In other words:

**The goal is not permanent relaxation.**

The goal is:

**greater flexibility in the relationship between cognition, situation, movement, and bodily state.**

### 17. Neuroplasticity Is Not Inherently Negative

The deeper principle is:

**Neuroplasticity itself is not the problem.**

What matters is which pattern is repeatedly reinforced.

If a movement is repeatedly performed with excessive tension:

**Movement → tension**

may become increasingly automatic.

But if the same movement is repeatedly performed in a relaxed and efficient state:

**Movement → relaxed execution**

may become increasingly available.

Thus, neuroplasticity is both:

**a mechanism that can reinforce dysfunctional patterns**

and

**a mechanism that can establish alternative patterns.**

The critical question becomes:

> ***What state are you repeatedly practicing?***

### 18. “The Same Amount of Reverse Practice” Is Not a Literal Formula

It would be too simplistic to say:

> *“If the dysfunctional pattern was practiced 1,000 times, practicing the opposite pattern 1,000 times will undo it.”*

Plasticity is influenced by many factors:

* repetition
* attention
* sensory feedback
* physiological state
* movement accuracy
* fatigue
* stress
* emotional significance
* successful experiences
* context
* competition between old and new patterns

Therefore, the more accurate statement is not:

**“Equal repetition cancels previous repetition.”**

It is:

**“The same principles of plasticity can potentially be used in the opposite direction.”**

### 19. Relaxation Alone May Potentially Be Enough to Produce Change

An additional hypothesis follows from the user’s experience.

It may not always be necessary to deliberately retrain every problematic movement.

If the broader tendency toward excessive tension can be reduced, then the person may simply stop repeatedly activating the old tension pattern.

For example:

**Specific cognition**
↓
Normally triggers tension
↓
But tension no longer appears
↓
The person performs the activity normally

Every successful experience becomes another instance in which:

**“This situation does not require tension.”**

The person may therefore be learning a new relationship without explicitly thinking of it as “rehabilitation.”

Simply **living more often in a non-tensed state** may itself alter the balance of learned responses.

This remains a hypothesis rather than an established treatment principle for neurological disorders.

### 20. The Overall Model

The complete model can be summarized as follows.

### Original learning

**Specific situation / cognition**
↓
Prediction of action
↓
Bodily tension
↓
Muscular contraction / physiological arousal
↓
Action performed in that state
↓
Repeated experience
↓
Neuroplasticity
↓
**“This cognition = tension” becomes increasingly automatic**

Eventually:

**The person may become tense before consciously deciding to tense.**

### Reverse learning

**Specific situation / cognition**
↓
Establish relaxation before the tension response begins
↓
Perform the action while maintaining that relaxed state
↓
Experience that the action can succeed without excessive tension
↓
Repeated experience
↓
A competing bodily pattern becomes available
↓
The automatic tension response may weaken

And if this approach generalizes broadly:

**More of everyday life is experienced without unnecessary tension.**

The body may gradually become less dependent on tension as its default response to particular cognitions and situations.

### 21. The Central Idea

The core of this model is not:

> ***“Stop being tense.”***

It is:

> ***“Learn to use the opposite state.”***

And even more precisely:

> ***“Instead of trying not to become tense, experience that the action can be performed while the body remains relaxed.”***

If tension can be learned, relaxation can potentially be learned as well.

If a particular cognition can become associated with tension, another bodily state can potentially become associated with that same cognition.

From this perspective, neuroplasticity is not simply a mechanism that creates bad habits.

It is a mechanism that strengthens **whatever body–cognition pattern is repeatedly experienced**.

The deeper question therefore becomes:

> ***What state is being practiced?***

At the same time, focal dystonia and other neurological movement disorders should not be reduced to this model alone. They involve complex neurological mechanisms and require appropriate medical assessment and treatment. The present framework is best understood as a conceptual model for thinking about the common ground between learned bodily tension, yips, performance-related tension, and everyday conditioned physical responses.
