### 0. About This Document

This document organizes a personal body-sensation model developed through long-term observation of how bodily tension, relaxation, posture, breathing, and internal sensations interact.

The word “relaxation” here does **not** simply mean muscle relaxation or becoming physically limp.

Instead, it refers to a state in which:

* unnecessary tension is reduced,
* deeper tissues regain an appropriate sense of cohesion,
* the body’s natural rhythmic fluctuations can propagate through the tissues,
* and the body does not need to be forcibly manipulated through breathing or conscious muscular control.

The concepts of a “capillary sponge,” “internal flow,” and “pulse synchronization” are metaphors and hypotheses used to describe subjective bodily experience. They should not be interpreted as literal descriptions of human physiology.

### 1. The Basic Idea

The human body can be imagined, phenomenologically, as a kind of **living three-dimensional sponge permeated by an extensive network of capillaries**.

Rather than viewing the body simply as a collection of muscles and bones, this model emphasizes the complex three-dimensional relationship between:

* muscles,
* connective tissues,
* blood vessels,
* tissue fluids,
* nerves,
* fascia,
* and subtle mechanical movements.

From this perspective, bodily state is not simply a binary distinction between:

> *tense vs. relaxed*

A more useful question is:

> ***Is the tissue in a state where internal movement and flow can occur naturally?***

### 2. Relaxation Is Not the Same as Letting Go

Ordinary descriptions of relaxation often imply:

* releasing muscle tension,
* dropping the shoulders,
* becoming softer,
* breathing deeply,
* or becoming generally relaxed.

But this model suggests that these are not necessarily the same thing.

The body can potentially exist in several different states.

### A. Excessive compression

The tissue is overly contracted or compressed.

This can be imagined as squeezing a sponge tightly.

The internal spaces become compressed, and the subjective sensation may be one of restricted movement or stagnation.

### B. Excessive expansion

The opposite can also occur.

If the body is intentionally expanded or excessively “opened,” the tissue may lose its sense of cohesion.

It can feel relaxed, but without a stable center or internal organization.

### C. Appropriate cohesion

The most important state is somewhere between these extremes.

The body is:

* not hard,
* not rigid,
* not collapsed,
* not excessively expanded,

but gently and naturally organized.

This can be described as:

> ***a sponge returning to an appropriate density.***

### 3. The Core of the Sponge Model

A sponge is not better simply because it is softer.

Nor is it better because it is more expanded.

What matters is whether its internal structure allows fluid and deformation to move through it naturally.

The same conceptual distinction is applied to the body.

The goal is therefore not:

> *“Make the body as relaxed as possible.”*

Instead:

> ***Reduce the factors that interfere with the body’s natural internal movement.***

These factors may include:

* excessive muscular tension,
* local fixation,
* unnecessary postural control,
* excessive breathing manipulation,
* intentional muscular control,
* or the impulse to constantly “correct” the body.

Relaxation therefore becomes less of an active technique and more of a process of **removing unnecessary interference**.

### 4. The Feeling of Being “Saturated” or “Insufficient”

The body is constantly undergoing internal movement.

In this model, the question is not simply whether there is “more blood” or “less blood.”

Instead, the relevant subjective question is:

> ***Can this tissue receive and circulate internal movement naturally?***

When tissue is:

* excessively compressed,
* excessively expanded,
* locally over-tensioned,
* or held in a fixed configuration,

the person may experience sensations such as:

* stagnation,
* heaviness,
* blockage,
* internal pressure,
* or a lack of movement.

The words “saturated” and “insufficient” are therefore phenomenological descriptions, not medical measurements of blood volume.

They describe a subjective sense that the tissue is either unable to accommodate internal movement or lacks sufficient cohesion for that movement to circulate naturally.

### 5. Why the Perineum, Pelvic Floor, and Deep Abdomen Are Useful Areas to Observe

Certain regions make this phenomenon easier to notice.

Examples include:

* the perineum,
* pelvic floor,
* lower abdomen,
* the area deep behind the navel,
* deeper abdominal structures,
* deep lumbar tissues,
* and the region around the diaphragm.

These areas are difficult to manipulate directly from the surface.

Simply trying to “relax the muscle” may not produce the desired state.

Instead, when unnecessary tension decreases, one may begin to perceive something different:

> ***A deep region that feels gently cohesive while remaining soft and internally mobile.***

The sensation is neither deliberate contraction nor complete collapse.

It is closer to:

> ***the tissue naturally settling into itself.***

### 6. The Role of Pulsation

One of the most important observations in this model is the relationship between bodily relaxation and **pulsation**.

Rather than deliberately moving the body, the deeper tissues may appear to organize themselves subtly in synchrony with the body’s pulse.

The subjective distinction is important.

There is a difference between:

> *“I am contracting this muscle.”*

and:

> *“The tissue seems to gather by itself in response to the pulse.”*

The second sensation is not experienced as an intentional muscular action.

It feels more like the body is revealing a rhythmic movement that was already present.

### 7. Pulse Synchronization Rather Than Forced Movement

When attention is placed on a deep region such as the perineum or the area behind the navel, a subtle rhythmic pattern may become noticeable.

Rather than intentionally contracting:

> *pulse arrives → tissue subtly gathers → pulse passes → tissue releases*

The movement is extremely small.

The important point is not the size of the movement but the **relationship between the movement and the pulse**.

The body seems to organize itself around an internally generated rhythm rather than being driven by deliberate muscular control.

### 8. Why Breathing Is Different

Breathing creates a large and obvious rhythmic movement throughout the body.

This makes breathing a powerful tool for changing bodily sensations.

For example:

* inhalation can create expansion,
* exhalation can create release or contraction.

This can make breathing useful as an entry point for learning bodily relaxation.

However, relying too heavily on breathing can introduce another problem:

> ***The person begins to move the body through breathing rather than allowing the body to organize itself naturally.***

The sequence becomes:

> *breathing → deliberate bodily movement → relaxation sensation*

Whereas the model described here is closer to:

> *reduce unnecessary tension
↓
tissue resistance decreases
↓
internal pulsation becomes easier to perceive
↓
tissue naturally organizes around the pulsation
↓
breathing no longer needs to drive the process*

Thus the goal is not necessarily to use breathing to create relaxation.

It is to create enough freedom for the body’s own rhythms to become apparent.

### 9. Why Conscious Breathing Can Become “Too One-Sided”

Breathing is a relatively large mechanical movement.

When consciously manipulated, attention can become biased toward:

* inhalation,
* exhalation,
* chest expansion,
* abdominal expansion,
* diaphragm movement,
* or deliberately deep breathing.

This can push the body toward a particular mechanical pattern.

In other words:

> *Conscious breathing uses a natural rhythm, but it can also impose a direction onto that rhythm.*

Pulse-based observation is different.

The pulse is smaller, continuous, and generated internally.

Therefore, it may encourage a different relationship:

> ***Rather than making the body move, allow the body to move according to its own rhythm.***

### 10. “The Body Naturally Gathers Around the Pulse”

The key sensation is not simply “feeling the pulse.”

The more interesting phenomenon is:

> ***The deeper tissues seem to organize themselves around the pulse without deliberate effort.***

For example:

* intentionally tightening the pelvic floor creates a muscular contraction,
* intentionally relaxing it can make the tissue collapse,
* but neither may correspond to the desired state.

The third state is:

> *The pulse arrives, the deep tissue subtly gathers, the pulse passes, and the tissue naturally returns.*

This is not experienced as an intentional exercise.

The sense of “I am doing something” becomes weaker.

### 11. Relaxation as “Subtracting Friction”

The simplest way to describe the overall approach may be:

> ***Relaxation is not adding a new movement. It is subtracting friction.***

Instead of adding:

* more breathing,
* more posture control,
* more muscular adjustment,
* more attention,
* more effort,

the practitioner gradually removes:

* unnecessary tension,
* local fixation,
* excessive muscular control,
* forced breathing,
* deliberate postural correction,
* and the impulse to manipulate internal sensations.

Then naturally occurring processes become easier to perceive.

These may include:

* cardiac pulsation,
* respiration,
* subtle muscular activity,
* elastic properties of tissue,
* postural adjustments,
* and autonomic fluctuations.

The fundamental shift is therefore:

> ***Do not force the body to relax. Remove what prevents the body from relaxing naturally.***

### 12. Relaxed Does Not Mean Weak

A key distinction in this model is:

> ***Relaxation and weakness are not the same thing.***

If relaxation simply meant eliminating muscular activity, the body would become unstable.

The state being described here is different.

Unnecessary surface tension decreases while deeper structural cohesion remains.

Therefore, the subjective goal is:

* less unnecessary effort,
* stable posture,
* preserved movement,
* preserved ability to generate force,
* clear mental functioning,
* and easier perception of internal bodily movement.

In other words:

> ***The body can be relaxed without being collapsed.***

### 13. Low Baseline Tension and On-Demand Output

The objective is not to keep the body permanently activated.

Instead:

> ***Reduce unnecessary baseline output while preserving the ability to produce output when needed.***

This can be described as:

> *low resident tension + preserved on-demand output*

rather than:

> *constant relaxation or constant activation.*

The body does not need to maintain a high level of readiness at all times.

The goal is to avoid carrying the energetic cost of “preparing for action” when no action is required.

### 14. Relaxation and Alertness Can Coexist

Another important implication is that bodily relaxation does not necessarily have to produce sleepiness.

The body may become:

* quieter,
* less tense,
* less noisy,

while the brain remains:

* alert,
* responsive,
* clear,
* and capable of high output.

This separates two concepts that are often implicitly linked:

> *physical tension = alertness*

and

> *physical relaxation = sleepiness.*

They do not necessarily have to be the same variable.

The body can potentially operate at low mechanical noise while the nervous system remains sufficiently active for cognition and action.

### 15. Stillness Makes the Sensation Easier to Detect

Large movements can obscure subtle internal movements.

During walking or exercise, the entire body is already moving.

In stillness, however, very small rhythmic changes become easier to notice.

Useful contexts may therefore include:

* sitting quietly,
* standing,
* slow walking,
* or simply remaining still without deliberately correcting posture.

The important principle is:

> ***Do less, and observe what the body does when it is not being constantly adjusted.***

### 16. Posture as an Emergent Result

Posture does not necessarily need to be constructed consciously.

A common approach is:

* straighten the spine,
* lower the shoulders,
* engage the abdomen,
* position the pelvis,
* stabilize the neck.

But every correction introduces another intentional action.

This can create additional tension.

An alternative is to observe:

> *Where does the body feel compressed?
Where is it excessively expanded?
Where does it naturally begin to organize itself?*

From this perspective:

> ***Posture is not something to construct first. It can emerge as a consequence of improved internal organization.***

### 17. The Difference Between Tightening, Collapsing, and Cohesion

Three states can be distinguished.

### Tightening

A deliberate muscular contraction.

### Collapsing

A reduction of tension without sufficient structural organization.

### Cohesion

The deeper tissues feel naturally organized and connected without requiring deliberate contraction.

The third state is the target of this model.

It can be described as:

> ***the body settling into itself without being forced into position.***

This does not necessarily mean that a specific muscle is contracting or that a particular anatomical structure is moving.

It is primarily a phenomenological description of the overall organization of bodily tension.

### 18. The Overall Process

The model can be represented as:

The body is experienced as a three-dimensional biological sponge
↓
Excessive compression or excessive expansion interferes with natural internal movement
↓
Unnecessary tension and fixation are reduced
↓
The tissues return toward an appropriate degree of cohesion
↓
Deep tissues begin to feel naturally organized
↓
Internal pulsation becomes easier to perceive
↓
Tissue movement begins to synchronize subtly with pulsation
↓
Large breathing-driven movements become less necessary
↓
The body can follow its own rhythms
↓
The need to consciously “relax” decreases
↓
The body becomes quieter without losing functional output

### 19. Why This Can Feel Like a Fundamental Change

This approach is difficult to grasp because conventional body training often begins with:

> ***How do I move my muscles correctly?***

The model described here asks a different question:

> ***What condition allows the body to move itself naturally?***

This changes the level of control.

Instead of:

> *consciously controlling muscles
↓
controlling posture
↓
controlling breathing*

the focus shifts toward:

> ***reducing unnecessary interference with the body’s internal organization.***

The subjective result may feel much larger than ordinary flexibility or relaxation.

The sensation of “a different dimension” does not need to imply anything supernatural.

It can simply mean:

> ***The basic strategy for interacting with the body has changed.***

The shift is from:

> *“I move my body.”*

to:

> ***“I create the conditions under which the body can organize itself.”***

### 20. Phenomenological Markers

Possible subjective markers of this state include:

* less need to deliberately relax,
* deeper tissues feeling naturally cohesive,
* internal movement becoming easier to perceive,
* pulsation becoming more apparent,
* less local rigidity,
* stable posture without excessive effort,
* preserved mental clarity,
* preserved ability to generate force,
* reduced need for deliberate breathing control,
* and a gradual disappearance of the feeling that one is “doing a relaxation exercise.”

One particularly important marker is:

> ***The less the person tries to create the state, the more naturally the state appears.***

### 21. A Note on Physiology

The “capillary sponge,” “internal flow,” and “pulse synchronization” concepts are not established physiological mechanisms as described here.

They are conceptual models for organizing subjective experience.

Actual human physiology involves complex interactions among:

* blood flow,
* vascular dynamics,
* tissue fluid,
* lymphatic circulation,
* muscle activity,
* fascia and connective tissue,
* respiration,
* cardiac pulsation,
* venous return,
* autonomic regulation,
* and neural processing.

Therefore:

> *“I feel this sensation” does not automatically mean “this specific physiological mechanism is occurring.”*

The useful question is instead:

> ***Can this phenomenological model reliably describe and predict changes in subjective bodily state?***

That can potentially be investigated through repeated observation and, where appropriate, physiological measurements.

### 22. Questions for Future Observation

The model can be developed further by observing:

1. Which body regions most reliably produce the sensation.
2. How clearly the sensation synchronizes with cardiac pulsation.
3. How it differs from deliberate breathing-based relaxation.
4. How posture affects the sensation.
5. Differences between stillness and movement.
6. The relationship between bodily relaxation and alertness.

8. Whether the state can be maintained for long periods.
9. Whether preserved output can coexist with reduced baseline tension.
10. Whether the sensation becomes increasingly automatic with practice.

### 23. Core Principle

The entire model can be summarized as:

> ***Do not forcibly relax the body. Create the conditions in which the body can naturally organize itself around its own rhythms.***

Or, more specifically:

> ***Think of the body as a living sponge. Do not squeeze it, and do not force it open. Reduce the friction that prevents it from naturally gathering and moving with the pulse.***

The ultimate shift is:

> ***From “relaxing the body”
to “stopping interference with the body’s natural relaxation.”***

That distinction is the central idea of this body-sensation model.
